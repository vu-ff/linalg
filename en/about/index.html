About this site

The companion to Linear Algebra for Physicists — from vector spaces to hilbert spaces.

What is here

3 lectures with 12 definitions, 18 named results, 17 proofs, 32 worked examples and 5 figures, plus 94 problems — 71 of them with a final answer you can check against your own. Everything is generated from the same LaTeX source as the printed notes, so the site and the PDFs cannot drift apart.

Languages

The site is published in English and Lithuanian, and the switcher in the header keeps you on the same page when the other language has it. Both are generated from their own LaTeX sources rather than translated in the browser, so a Lithuanian page is the Lithuanian notes, with its own figures and its own PDFs — not the English ones passed through a machine.

A lecture appears in a language only once that language's translation has been brought up to date with the current text. Until then it is named in the course roadmap and cannot be opened, which is deliberate: a stale translation of a corrected proof is worse than no translation at all.

Hints, answers, solutions

The course's LaTeX already draws the line this site follows. A hint is a nudge, an answer is the terminal result with no reasoning, and a solution is the full worked method. The style package's own student edition shows the first two and not the third; so does this site. Worked solutions are done in the tutorial, which is where the learning in them actually happens, and they are stripped from the source before the site is built rather than merely hidden from it.

So: get stuck properly first, take a hint if you need one, and look at the answer only once you have one of your own to compare it with. An answer that agrees with yours tells you something. An answer you read first tells you nothing at all.

The laboratories

Each lecture carries a model you can change — a matrix whose eigendirections you can hunt for, a Gram–Schmidt construction you can step through, a spectrum you can perturb. Each one comes with a short guided sequence that asks you to predict what will happen before it sets the model up. That order is deliberate. A model you watch move teaches very little; a model you consult because you committed to an answer and want to know why it was wrong teaches a great deal.

The self-check

Each lecture ends with a short set of questions on the ideas it is most often misread on. They are for finding out whether you have understood, not for marks: nothing is recorded anywhere but your own browser, and the explanation is shown whether you were right or wrong, because the explanation is the point. Take the hint before you answer if you want it — a hint that arrives once you already know the answer teaches nothing.

Your progress

Ticked objectives and self-check answers are stored in your own browser and nowhere else. There is no account, no login, and nothing is sent anywhere — which also means your progress does not follow you to another device, and clearing your browser data clears it.

Found a mistake?

Please report it. Corrections during a course's first run are genuinely valuable — bring it to a lecture or tutorial and it will be fixed at source and republished.

Colophon

Built as a static site from LaTeX. Pandoc preserves the notes' own definition, theorem, example and remark structure; KaTeX renders the mathematics at build time, using the course's own notation macros translated automatically from the same style file the PDFs load; and every figure is the same TikZ drawing as in the PDF, compiled to SVG rather than screenshotted. No tracking, no cookies, no third-party requests.
