Last lecture a vector space was a set equipped with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the vector-space axioms; a subset of an ambient vector space was a subspace exactly when it was closed under linear combinations. Today we turn linear combinations into the two most useful concepts in the subject. A basis is a minimal list of vectors from which every other vector is built in exactly one way; it is the abstract version of a coordinate system. The number of vectors in a basis is the dimension, the right notion of “size” for a vector space.

The pay-off is concrete. Once we fix a basis, every abstract vector—a polynomial, a matrix, a quantum state—becomes an honest column of numbers, and every linear-algebra computation reduces to arithmetic you already know. In quantum mechanics, choosing a basis is exactly what a physicist means by choosing a representation. Here ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ is only an early label for the vector ψ\psiψ; Dirac notation is introduced formally in Lecture 7. Finite or discrete bases give ordinary coordinate columns. Position and momentum wavefunctions are the later continuous/generalized analogue, not finite columns in ordinary Hilbert bases. By the end of today you will be able to find bases, compute dimensions, pass between a vector and its coordinates, and count dimensions of sums and intersections.

Linear independence

A spanning list may carry redundancy: some vector may already be a combination of the others. The lists with no redundancy are the important ones.

The fundamental counting theorem

Everything that follows—that dimension is well defined, that bases behave as coordinate systems—rests on one inequality.

Theorem 2.12 (Independent ≤\le≤ spanning). In a vector space VVV, the length of any linearly independent list is at most the length of any spanning list.

Proof. Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be independent and w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ span VVV; we prove m≤nm\le nm≤n. We run a process of mmm steps, each inserting one uuu and deleting one www, keeping a spanning list of length nnn throughout.

Step 1. The list w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ spans VVV, so adjoining u1u_1u1​ in front gives the dependent list u1,w1,…,wnu_1,w_1,\dots,w_nu1​,w1​,…,wn​ (here u1u_1u1​ lies in the span of the www’s). By Lemma 2.10 some entry is in the span of the earlier ones; it is not u1u_1u1​, since independence makes u1≠0u_1\neq0u1​=0 and span⁡( )={0}\spn(\,)=\set{0}span()={0}. So it is some wkw_kwk​, which we delete. The result is a spanning list of length nnn containing u1u_1u1​.

Step kkk (2≤k≤m)(2\le k\le m)(2≤k≤m). Suppose we hold a spanning list of length nnn containing u1,…,uk−1u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}u1​,…,uk−1​. Insert uku_kuk​ just after uk−1u_{k-1}uk−1​: the new list is dependent (every added vector lies in the span of a spanning list). By Lemma 2.10 some entry is in the span of the earlier ones. It cannot be any of u1,…,uku_1,\dots,u_ku1​,…,uk​, for those are independent and so none lies in the span of the previous uuu’s. Hence it is one of the remaining www’s, which we delete, restoring a spanning list of length nnn that now contains u1,…,uku_1,\dots,u_ku1​,…,uk​.

If m>nm>nm>n, then after Step nnn we hold a spanning list of length nnn consisting of u1,…,unu_1,\dots,u_nu1​,…,un​ only (all www’s gone). Step n+1n+1n+1 inserts un+1u_{n+1}un+1​, producing a dependent list of uuu’s alone; Lemma 2.10 then puts some uuu in the span of earlier uuu’s, contradicting independence. Therefore m≤nm\le nm≤n. 

A useful by-product is that every subspace UUU of a finite-dimensional space VVV is itself finite-dimensional. Repeatedly choose a vector of UUU outside the span of those already chosen. The chosen vectors stay independent—each is outside the span of its predecessors, which is exactly Lemma 2.10 read backwards—so Theorem 2.12 caps their number at dim⁡V\dim VdimV and the process must stop. It can only stop when no vector of UUU lies outside the current span, that is, when the chosen list spans UUU.

Bases and dimension

Definition 2.13 (Basis). A basis of VVV is a list that is linearly independent and spans VVV.

Theorem 2.14 (Bases give unique coordinates). A list e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ is a basis of VVV if and only if every v∈Vv\in Vv∈V has a unique expression v=a1e1+⋯+anenv=a_1e_1+\cdots+a_ne_nv=a1​e1​+⋯+an​en​ with ak∈Fa_k\in\Fak​∈F.

Proof. (⇒\Rightarrow⇒) Spanning gives existence; independence gives uniqueness, by the remark in §2. (⇐\Leftarrow⇐) Existence of such expressions for every vvv says the list spans. A basis can always be produced, in two complementary ways: by trimming a spanning list down, or by growing an independent list up.

Theorem 2.16 (Reduction to a basis). Every spanning list of VVV can be reduced to a basis of VVV by deleting some of its vectors.

Proof. While the list is linearly dependent, Lemma 2.10 supplies a vector lying in the span of the earlier ones; delete it, which by the same lemma leaves the span equal to VVV. Each deletion shortens a finite list, so the process halts, and it can halt only when the list is independent—an independent spanning list, i.e. a basis. 

Theorem 2.17 (Extension to a basis). In a finite-dimensional space, every linearly independent list extends to a basis.

Proof. Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be independent and w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ a basis. Apply Theorem 2.16 to the spanning list u1,…,um,w1,…,wnu_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_nu1​,…,um​,w1​,…,wn​. No uiu_iui​ is ever deleted: the vectors preceding it are u1,…,ui−1u_1,\dots,u_{i-1}u1​,…,ui−1​, which are independent, so uiu_iui​ is not in their span and is not a candidate for removal. Only www’s are discarded, leaving a basis that still contains u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​. 

Corollary 2.18 (Existence of a basis). Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis—reduce any finite spanning list.

For dimension to make sense, all bases of a space must have the same length. Theorem 2.12 delivers exactly this.

Theorem 2.21 (Basis length is well defined). Any two bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same length.

Proof. Let B1,B2\mathcal B_1,\mathcal B_2B1​,B2​ be bases. Since B1\mathcal B_1B1​ is independent and B2\mathcal B_2B2​ spans, Theorem 2.12 gives len⁡B1≤len⁡B2\operatorname{len}\mathcal B_1\le\operatorname{len}\mathcal B_2lenB1​≤lenB2​. Swapping their roles gives the reverse inequality, so the lengths are equal. 

Definition 2.22 (Dimension). The dimension dim⁡V\dim VdimV of a finite-dimensional vector space is the length of any basis.

Example 2.23 (Dimensions). dim⁡Fn=n\dim\F^n=ndimFn=n;  dim⁡Pm(F)=m+1\dim\Poly_m(\F)=m+1dimPm​(F)=m+1;  dim⁡Mm×n(F)=mn\dim M_{m\times n}(\F)=mndimMm×n​(F)=mn. Two shortcuts make checking a basis far easier once the dimension is known; both follow from Theorem 2.12.

Proposition 2.24 (Right length is enough). Let dim⁡V=n\dim V=ndimV=n. Then any linearly independent list of length nnn is a basis, and any spanning list of length nnn is a basis.

Proof. An independent list of length nnn extends to a basis by Theorem 2.17; but every basis has length nnn, so no vector is added and the list is already a basis. A spanning list of length nnn reduces to a basis by Theorem 2.16; again every basis has length nnn, so no vector is removed and the list is already a basis.

For a finite or discrete basis a basis expansion reads ∣ψ⟩=∑kak∣ek⟩\ket{\psi}=\sum_k a_k\ket{e_k}∣ψ⟩=∑k​ak​∣ek​⟩, and the coordinate vector (a1,…,an)(a_1,\dots,a_n)(a1​,…,an​) is the “wavefunction” of the state in that basis. In the continuous position or momentum representation, the analogue is a function and requires the Hilbert-space machinery of Lectures 13–14. Changing representation changes the coordinates while leaving the represented pure-state ray untouched. Linear algebra is the bookkeeping that makes such changes routine.

The dimension formula for sums

Recall from Lecture 1 that U+WU+WU+W and U∩WU\cap WU∩W are subspaces. Their dimensions are linked by a counting formula, analogous to inclusion–exclusion for sets.

Corollary 2.33 (Direct sums add dimensions). dim⁡(U⊕W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W\dim(U\dsum W)=\dim U+\dim Wdim(U⊕W)=dimU+dimW. More generally, U+WU+WU+W is direct if and only if dim⁡(U+W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim Wdim(U+W)=dimU+dimW. Proof. By Theorem 2.32, dim⁡(U+W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim Wdim(U+W)=dimU+dimW exactly when dim⁡(U∩W)=0\dim(U\cap W)=0dim(U∩W)=0, i.e. U∩W={0}U\cap W=\set{0}U∩W={0}, which is the two-subspace criterion for directness from Lecture 1. 

Example 2.34 (Two planes must meet). If U,WU,WU,W are two-dimensional subspaces (planes through 000) of R3\R^3R3, then dim⁡(U∩W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W−dim⁡(U+W)≥2+2−3=1\dim(U\cap W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U+W)\ge 2+2-3=1dim(U∩W)=dimU+dimW−dim(U+W)≥2+2−3=1. The span of a list is the smallest subspace containing it. A list is independent when 000 has only the trivial representation, and a basis is an independent spanning list—equivalently, a list giving every vector unique coordinates. Every linearly independent list is no longer than any spanning list (Theorem 2.12); hence all bases share a length, the dimension. In an nnn-dimensional space, an independent or spanning list of length nnn is automatically a basis. Fixing an ordered basis turns each vector into a coordinate column in Fn\F^nFn; in a finite/discrete model this is what a physicist calls choosing a representation. Continuous position and momentum representations instead produce wavefunctions and require the later Hilbert-space framework. Finally, dim⁡(U+W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W−dim⁡(U∩W)\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)dim(U+W)=dimU+dimW−dim(U∩W), with equality to dim⁡U+dim⁡W\dim U+\dim WdimU+dimW exactly for direct sums.

  • Span—all linear combinations of a list; the smallest subspace containing it.
  • Linear independence—only the trivial combination yields 000.
  • Basis—an independent spanning list; gives every vector unique coordinates.
  • Dimension—the common length of all bases (independent ≤\le≤ spanning).
  • Coordinate vector—the column [v]B∈Fn[v]_{\mathcal B}\in\F^n[v]B​∈Fn once a finite ordered basis is fixed; continuous representations are function-valued analogues.
  • Dimension formula—dim⁡(U+W)=dim⁡U+dim⁡W−dim⁡(U∩W)\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)dim(U+W)=dimU+dimW−dim(U∩W); additive exactly for direct sums.

Self-check

6 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1How do you test whether w lies in span(v₁,…,vₖ)?

Span consists of all linear combinations.

Membership is exactly the existence of coefficients solving the displayed vector equation.

2A list containing the zero vector is always…

Give the zero vector a non-zero coefficient and all others coefficient zero.

That choice gives a non-trivial linear combination equal to zero, so the list is dependent.

3Which properties ensure that every vector has exactly one coordinate expansion in a given list?

One property gives existence and the other gives uniqueness.

Spanning supplies an expression and independence prevents two different expressions.

4What is dim P₄(R), the polynomials of degree at most 4?

Use the standard basis 1,t,…,t⁴.

The five polynomials 1,t,t²,t³,t⁴ form a basis.

5V has dimension 3. What follows for any independent list of three vectors in V?

An independent list of the full dimension already has the right length.

In a 3-dimensional space, three independent vectors automatically span and form a basis.

6dim U=3, dim W=2, and dim(U∩W)=1. What is dim(U+W)?

Add the dimensions and subtract the overlap once.

dim(U+W)=3+2−1=4.
