We have the objects of linear algebra—vector spaces—and their coordinate systems—bases. Now we study the maps between vector spaces that respect this structure. A linear map is a function that commutes with addition and scalar multiplication; equivalently, it preserves linear combinations, so it preserves superposition.

This is also why linear maps are the right notion of a “transformation” in physics. In quantum mechanics linear operators do three different jobs. One kind of operator encodes a measurable quantity. A second describes how a closed system evolves in time. A third describes a symmetry. Measurement itself does something that is not a linear operation: it selects the part of a state belonging to one possible outcome and replaces the state by that part. We will define all of this in Lectures 7–10, and nothing in the present lecture depends on it.

Two subspaces measure how a linear map behaves: its kernel (what it sends to zero) and its image (what it can produce). The central result of the lecture, the rank–nullity theorem, ties their dimensions together in a single equation dim⁡V=dim⁡ker⁡T+dim⁡im⁡T\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\img TdimV=dimkerT+dimimT, and from it we will read off when linear systems are solvable and when operators are invertible.

A linear map is determined by a basis

A linear map cannot be chosen freely at every point: (3.1) ties its values together. In fact its values on a single basis fix it completely, and those values may be assigned arbitrarily.

The algebra of linear maps

Linear maps can be added, scaled, and composed.

Remark 3.12. Kernel and injectivity

The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps

The kernel records what TTT destroys; the image records what it builds. The next theorem—the central result of the lecture—says their dimensions exactly account for the dimension of the domain (Figure 1).

Theorem 3.24 (Rank–nullity). Suppose VVV is finite-dimensional and T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W). Then im⁡T\img TimT is finite-dimensional and dim⁡V=dim⁡ker⁡T+dim⁡im⁡T.\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\img T .dimV=dimkerT+dimimT.

Proof. Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be a basis of ker⁡T\ker TkerT, so dim⁡ker⁡T=m\dim\ker T=mdimkerT=m. We show Tv1,…,TvnTv_1,\dots,Tv_nTv1​,…,Tvn​ is a basis of im⁡T\img TimT, which gives dim⁡im⁡T=n\dim\img T=ndimimT=n and finishes the count.

Spanning. Any v∈Vv\in Vv∈V is v=∑iaiui+∑jbjvjv=\sum_i a_iu_i+\sum_j b_jv_jv=∑i​ai​ui​+∑j​bj​vj​; applying TTT and using Tui=0Tu_i=0Tui​=0 leaves Tv=∑jbj TvjTv=\sum_j b_j\,Tv_jTv=∑j​bj​Tvj​. Hence Tv1,…,TvnTv_1,\dots,Tv_nTv1​,…,Tvn​ spans im⁡T\img TimT (and im⁡T\img TimT is finite-dimensional).

Then T(∑jcjvj)=0T\bigl(\sum_j c_jv_j\bigr)=0T(∑j​cj​vj​)=0, so ∑jcjvj∈ker⁡T\sum_j c_jv_j\in\ker T∑j​cj​vj​∈kerT and can be written ∑idiui\sum_i d_iu_i∑i​di​ui​. Two consequences follow with no further work, just by comparing dimensions.

Corollary 3.26 (Dimension obstructions). Let V,WV,WV,W be finite-dimensional and T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W).

  1. If dim⁡V>dim⁡W\dim V>\dim WdimV>dimW, then TTT is not injective.

Remark 3.34 (Forward preview: operators in quantum mechanics). Quotient spaces [self-study]

For a genuinely inhomogeneous system Ax=bAx=bAx=b with b≠0b\neq0b=0, a solution satisfies xp∉ker⁡Tx_p\notin\ker Txp​∈/kerT, so the solution set xp+ker⁡Tx_p+\ker Txp​+kerT does not contain 000 and is not a subspace. The right language for all these cosets is the quotient space.

One word is needed before the next result, ahead of its proper treatment. The First Isomorphism Theorem is the canonical form of rank–nullity: it does not merely count dimensions but exhibits an explicit isomorphism.

Remark 3.38 (Physics: gauge fields [self-study]). Quotient spaces appear at the foundations of modern physics. In electromagnetism the 444-potential AμA_\muAμ​ is not unique: two potentials related by Aμ→Aμ+∂μχA_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chiAμ​→Aμ​+∂μ​χ produce the same field strength Fμν=∂μAν−∂νAμF_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\muFμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​. The “pure gauge” configurations (those of the form ∂μχ\partial_\mu\chi∂μ​χ) form a subspace of the space of all potentials, and the space of physically distinct fields is the quotient. A gauge-fixed description chooses one representative from each coset—just as choosing xpx_pxp​ picks one solution from each coset xp+ker⁡Tx_p+\ker Txp​+kerT.

A linear map preserves linear combinations, hence superposition. It is fixed by its values on a basis, which may be chosen freely (Theorem 3.5). Its kernel ker⁡T\ker TkerT and image im⁡T\img TimT are subspaces; TTT is injective exactly when ker⁡T={0}\ker T=\set0kerT={0} and surjective exactly when im⁡T=W\img T=WimT=W. The rank–nullity theorem dim⁡V=dim⁡ker⁡T+dim⁡im⁡T\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\img TdimV=dimkerT+dimimT ties the two together, yielding at a glance that maps to smaller spaces cannot be injective, maps to larger spaces cannot be surjective, and operators on a finite-dimensional space are injective if and only if surjective. In coordinates this is the bookkeeping of pivots and free variables behind every linear system.

  • Linear map—preserves linear combinations (superposition); fixed by its values on a basis.
  • Kernel and image—subspaces ker⁡T\ker TkerT and im⁡T\img TimT; injective   ⟺  ker⁡T={0}\iff\ker T=\set0⟺kerT={0}, surjective   ⟺  im⁡T=W\iff\img T=W⟺imT=W.
  • Rank–nullity—dim⁡V=dim⁡ker⁡T+dim⁡im⁡T\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\img TdimV=dimkerT+dimimT.
  • Operator dichotomy—on a finite-dimensional space, injective   ⟺  \iff⟺ surjective.
  • Quotient space; First Isomorphism Theorem—V/ker⁡T≅im⁡TV/\ker T\iso\img TV/kerT≅imT (self-study); gauge equivalence in physics.
