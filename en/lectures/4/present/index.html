Lecture 4

Matrices of Linear Maps; Change of Basis

By the end of this lecture you will be able to

  • Build the matrix M(T)\Mat(T)M(T) in chosen bases and use [Tv]=M(T)[v][Tv]=\Mat(T)[v][Tv]=M(T)[v].
  • Explain why composition of maps is matrix multiplication.
  • Decide whether a map is invertible, and classify spaces up to isomorphism by dimension.
  • Change basis and recognize trace and determinant as similarity invariants.

In Lecture 3 we proved an important fact: a linear map is completely determined by the images of the basis vectors. If we also fix a basis of the codomain, we can write each of those images as a coordinate vector. Placing these coordinate vectors side by side as columns gives a matrix. In this lecture we turn the informal correspondence “linear map ↔\leftrightarrow↔ matrix” into a precise isomorphism. Three things follow. First, we do not choose the rule for multiplying matrices: it is exactly the rule that composition of linear maps demands. Second, an abstract space of linear maps is simply a space of matrices. Third, when we change basis, the matrix of the same operator changes, and the new and old matrices are related by a similarity transformation A′=P−1APA'=P^{-1}APA′=P−1AP.

For physics, this dependence on the basis is what matters most. In finite-dimensional (discrete) models, choosing a basis means choosing a coordinate representation. Discretized position, momentum and energy models are examples. Continuous position or momentum representations require the Hilbert-space machinery that we study in Lectures 13–14. In later lectures we will choose bases in which an operator’s matrix is as simple as possible. The simplest is a diagonal matrix — that will be the goal of the spectral theorem.

Composition becomes matrix multiplication

Matrices are added and multiplied by scalars entrywise. With these operations the map M\MatM respects the linear structure.

Proposition 4.8 (The map M\MatM is linear). For all maps S,T∈L(V,W)S,T\in\Lin(V,W)S,T∈L(V,W) and scalars a∈Fa\in\Fa∈F, M(S+T)=M(S)+M(T)andM(aT)=a M(T).\Mat(S+T)=\Mat(S)+\Mat(T)\quad\allowbreak \text{and}\quad\allowbreak \Mat(aT)=a\,\Mat(T).M(S+T)=M(S)+M(T)andM(aT)=aM(T).

Proof. Consider the kkk-th column of the matrix M(S+T)\Mat(S+T)M(S+T). It holds the coordinates of the vector (S+T)vk=Svk+Tvk(S+T)v_k=Sv_k+Tv_k(S+T)vk​=Svk​+Tvk​ in the basis C\mathcal CC. The coordinate map is linear, so this column equals the sum of the kkk-th columns of M(S)\Mat(S)M(S) and M(T)\Mat(T)M(T). The case of multiplication by a scalar is proved in the same way. 

What corresponds to the composition of maps in the language of matrices? The answer determines how matrices must be multiplied.

The map M\MatM from L(V,W)\Lin(V,W)L(V,W) to the matrix space Fm×n\F^{m\times n}Fm×n is linear. It is injective: if the matrix of a map is zero, then that map sends every basis vector to the zero vector, so the map itself is zero. The map M\MatM is also surjective: any matrix prescribes images of the basis vectors, and in Lecture 3 we saw that such images define a unique linear map. Hence M\MatM is a bijective linear map.

A bijective linear map is called an isomorphism, and spaces between which an isomorphism exists are called isomorphic (written V≅WV\iso WV≅W). We met the term briefly in Lecture 3’s subsection on quotient spaces. The next section defines an isomorphism through invertibility and proves that the two definitions agree. So M\MatM is an isomorphism. This lets us compute the dimension promised in Lecture 3.

Corollary 4.12 (L(V,W)≅Fm×n\Lin(V,W)\iso\F^{m\times n}L(V,W)≅Fm×n). The map M\MatM is an isomorphism from L(V,W)\Lin(V,W)L(V,W) onto Fm×n\F^{m\times n}Fm×n. Hence dim⁡L(V,W)=(dim⁡V)(dim⁡W).\dim\Lin(V,W)=(\dim V)(\dim W).dimL(V,W)=(dimV)(dimW).

Proposition 4.13 (Matrix rank is map rank). For any choice of bases, the dimension dim⁡im⁡T\dim\img TdimimT of the image equals the column rank of M(T)\Mat(T)M(T), that is, the dimension of the span of its columns.

Proof. The coordinate map from WWW to Fm\F^mFm, w↦[w]Cw\mapsto[w]_{\mathcal C}w↦[w]C​, is linear and injective. Let u1,…,uru_1,\dots,u_ru1​,…,ur​ be a basis of the image im⁡T\img TimT, so r=dim⁡im⁡Tr=\dim\img Tr=dimimT. By Lecture 3’s proposition “Action on lists”, the vectors [u1]C,…,[ur]C[u_1]_{\mathcal C},\dots,[u_r]_{\mathcal C}[u1​]C​,…,[ur​]C​ span the column span and, since the map is injective, are linearly independent. Hence they form a basis of the column span, and the column rank equals r=dim⁡im⁡Tr=\dim\img Tr=dimimT. 

Remark 4.14. The image im⁡T\img TimT does not depend on the bases, so neither does the column rank. This result also connects map rank with Gaussian elimination. Reduce a matrix AAA to its reduced row-echelon form RRR. Every elementary row operation is invertible, so the equations Ax=0Ax=0Ax=0 and Rx=0Rx=0Rx=0 have the same solutions. Hence the columns of AAA and of RRR satisfy the same linear relations, and their column ranks agree. The pivot columns of RRR are linearly independent, and every column without a pivot is a linear combination of them. So if RRR has rrr pivots, the column rank of AAA equals rrr.

The same number rrr is also the row rank, the dimension of the span of the rows. Each row operation replaces rows by linear combinations of rows and is invertible, so the span of the rows does not change. The nonzero rows of RRR are linearly independent, since each has a pivot where the other rows have zeros. Hence the row rank of AAA is also rrr: row rank equals column rank. So Gaussian elimination computes the map rank dim⁡im⁡T\dim\img TdimimT, which does not depend on the bases.

Invertibility and isomorphisms

Definition 4.16 (Invertible map, isomorphism). A linear map T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W) is called invertible if there is a linear map S∈L(W,V)S\in\Lin(W,V)S∈L(W,V) such that ST=IVST=I_VST=IV​ and TS=IWTS=I_WTS=IW​. Such an SSS is unique; we write it T−1T^{-1}T−1. An invertible linear map is called an isomorphism. The spaces V,WV,WV,W are called isomorphic (written V≅WV\iso WV≅W) if there is an isomorphism between them.

The next proposition says that a linear map is invertible if and only if it is bijective. After it we prove the main fact of this section: whether two finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic is decided by a single number — the dimension.

Proposition 4.17 (Invertible === bijective). A linear map T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W) is invertible if and only if it is both injective and surjective. In that case its inverse function is automatically linear.

Proof. An invertible map has a two-sided inverse, so it is a bijection. Conversely, let TTT be a bijection and T−1T^{-1}T−1 its inverse function. Take any two vectors of WWW and write them as wi=Tviw_i=Tv_iwi​=Tvi​ (possible because TTT is surjective). Then for any a∈Fa\in\Fa∈F we have T−1(w1+aw2)=T−1(T(v1+av2))=v1+av2=T−1w1+a T−1w2,T^{-1}(w_1+aw_2)=T^{-1}\bigl(T(v_1+av_2)\bigr)=v_1+av_2 =T^{-1}w_1+a\,T^{-1}w_2 ,T−1(w1​+aw2​)=T−1(T(v1​+av2​))=v1​+av2​=T−1w1​+aT−1w2​, so T−1T^{-1}T−1 is linear. By construction it is a two-sided inverse. 

Theorem 4.18 (Dimension classifies spaces). Two finite-dimensional vector spaces over F\FF are isomorphic if and only if their dimensions agree.

Proof. Suppose T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W is an isomorphism. Then ker⁡T={0}\ker T=\set0kerT={0} and im⁡T=W\img T=WimT=W, so by rank–nullity dim⁡V=dim⁡im⁡T=dim⁡W\dim V=\dim\img T=\dim WdimV=dimimT=dimW.

Conversely, let dim⁡V=dim⁡W=n\dim V=\dim W=ndimV=dimW=n. Choose a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ of VVV and a basis w1,…,wnw_1,\dots,w_nw1​,…,wn​ of WWW. A single numerical determinant has settled an abstract question.

In the language of matrices: TTT is invertible if and only if its matrix M(T)\Mat(T)M(T) is an invertible square matrix. In that case M(T−1)=M(T)−1\Mat(T^{-1})=\Mat(T)^{-1}M(T−1)=M(T)−1. This follows directly from Theorem 4.9 applied to T−1T=IT^{-1}T=IT−1T=I.

Change of basis and similarity

The matrix of a map depends on the chosen bases. We now determine exactly how the matrix of an operator T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) changes when we pass from one basis of VVV to another. Let B=(v1,…,vn)\mathcal B=(v_1,\dots,v_n)B=(v1​,…,vn​) be the “old” basis and B′=(v1′,…,vn′)\mathcal B'=(v_1',\dots,v_n')B′=(v1′​,…,vn′​) the “new” one.

Definition 4.24 (Change-of-basis matrix). The change-of-basis matrix PPP is the n×nn\times nn×n matrix whose kkk-th column holds the coordinates of the new basis vector vk′v_k'vk′​ in the old basis B\mathcal BB. Equivalently, P=M(I,B′,B)P=\Mat(I,\mathcal B',\mathcal B)P=M(I,B′,B).

The matrix PPP is the matrix of the identity map, so by Theorem 4.6, for every vector vvv, [v]B=P[v]B′.(4.2) \tag{4.2} [v]_{\mathcal B}=P[v]_{\mathcal B'} . [v]B​=P[v]B′​.(4.2) So the change-of-basis matrix P=M(I,B′,B)P=\Mat(I,\mathcal B',\mathcal B)P=M(I,B′,B) carries coordinate vectors from the new basis B′\mathcal B'B′ to the old basis B\mathcal BB. The identity map is invertible, and so is PPP: its inverse P−1P^{-1}P−1 carries coordinate vectors from the old basis to the new one, [v]B′=P−1[v]B[v]_{\mathcal B'}=P^{-1}[v]_{\mathcal B}[v]B′​=P−1[v]B​.

Example 4.25 (Converting coordinates). Let B\mathcal BB be the standard basis of R2\R^2R2 and B′=((1,1),(1,−1))\mathcal B'=\bigl((1,1),(1,-1)\bigr)B′=((1,1),(1,−1)). Writing the new basis vectors as columns gives P=(111−1).P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.P=(11​1−1​). Let the coordinates of a vector vvv in the new basis be [v]B′=(3,1)[v]_{\mathcal B'}=(3,1)[v]B′​=(3,1). Check: 3(1,1)+1(1,−1)=(4,2)3(1,1)+1(1,-1)=(4,2)3(1,1)+1(1,−1)=(4,2). Note that when we multiply by matrices, we write coordinate vectors as columns.

Theorem 4.26 (Similarity). Let an operator T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) have matrices A=M(T,B)A=\Mat(T,\mathcal B)A=M(T,B) and A′=M(T,B′)A'=\Mat(T,\mathcal B')A′=M(T,B′), and let P=M(I,B′,B)P=\Mat(I,\mathcal B',\mathcal B)P=M(I,B′,B) be the change-of-basis matrix of Definition 4.24. Then A′=P−1AP.A'=P^{-1}AP .A′=P−1AP.

Proof. Take any vector vvv. Applying (4.2) and Theorem 4.6 repeatedly, we get A′[v]B′=[Tv]B′=P−1[Tv]B=P−1A [v]B=P−1AP [v]B′.A'[v]_{\mathcal B'}=[Tv]_{\mathcal B'}=P^{-1}[Tv]_{\mathcal B} =P^{-1}A\,[v]_{\mathcal B}=P^{-1}AP\,[v]_{\mathcal B'} .A′[v]B′​=[Tv]B′​=P−1[Tv]B​=P−1A[v]B​=P−1AP[v]B′​. This equality holds for all coordinate vectors [v]B′[v]_{\mathcal B'}[v]B′​, so A′=P−1APA'=P^{-1}APA′=P−1AP (Figure 2). 

Definition 4.27 (Similar matrices). Square matrices AAA and A′A'A′ are similar if there is an invertible matrix PPP such that A′=P−1APA'=P^{-1}APA′=P−1AP. Similar matrices are exactly the matrices of one operator in different bases.

Proposition 4.28 (Similarity invariants). Similarity is an equivalence relation, and similar matrices have the same trace and the same determinant.

Proof. Reflexivity (P=IP=IP=I), symmetry (A=PA′P−1A=PA'P^{-1}A=PA′P−1) and transitivity (multiply the change-of-basis matrices) are checked directly. For the trace, use the identity tr⁡(XY)=tr⁡(YX)\tr(XY)=\tr(YX)tr(XY)=tr(YX): tr⁡(P−1AP)=tr⁡(A(PP−1))=tr⁡A.\tr(P^{-1}AP)=\tr\bigl(A(PP^{-1})\bigr)=\tr A.tr(P−1AP)=tr(A(PP−1))=trA. For the determinant, det⁡(P−1AP)=det⁡(P)−1det⁡(A)det⁡(P)=det⁡A.\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det(P)^{-1}\det(A)\det(P)=\det A. det(P−1AP)=det(P)−1det(A)det(P)=detA. 

Hence trace and determinant are properties of the operator, not of any particular matrix of it. In Lecture 5 we will see that the same holds for eigenvalues: similar matrices have the same eigenvalues. This is why diagonalization makes sense.

Remark 4.29 (Operators and general maps). Similarity compares two matrices of one operator, so the same basis is used on the domain and on the codomain. For a general map T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W) the bases can be changed independently: by a matrix PPP on VVV and by a matrix QQQ on WWW. The corresponding formula is then A′=Q−1APA'=Q^{-1}APA′=Q−1AP, and such matrices are called equivalent. Equivalence is weaker than similarity: the matrix of every rank-rrr map is equivalent to the block matrix with an r×rr\times rr×r identity matrix in the top-left corner and zeros elsewhere. This normal form can be reached with facts we have already proved. Choose a basis of VVV whose last vectors span ker⁡T\ker TkerT. A basis of WWW consists of the images of the first basis vectors, extended to a basis. We do not carry out this construction here. Similarity preserves more information — trace, determinant, eigenvalues. These quantities make sense only when the domain and the codomain coincide.

Example 4.30 (The matrix of a reflection in a suitable basis). Let T∈L(R2)T\in\Lin(\R^2)T∈L(R2) be the reflection across the line y=xy=xy=x, that is, T(x,y)=(y,x)T(x,y)=(y,x)T(x,y)=(y,x). The map, not the matrix

A matrix is only a record of a linear map in a chosen basis, not the map itself. Quantities that do not change under a change of basis — rank, trace, determinant, and soon eigenvalues — are properties of the operator itself. Everything else depends on the chosen coordinates. In the coming lectures we will use this freedom: we will look for a basis in which the matrix is diagonal, so that the action of the operator becomes clear. In quantum mechanics one often passes to a basis made of states of definite energy. In this basis the evolution operator e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ is diagonal, so each basis state only acquires a phase factor as time goes on.

Remark 4.34 (Forward preview: density matrices). In later lectures we study finite-dimensional quantum models. In them a state is described by a density matrix ρ\rhoρ — a positive operator of trace one — and an observable by a self-adjoint operator AAA. Under a change of basis, the matrices of both operators change in the same way: ρ′=P−1ρPandA′=P−1AP.\rho'=P^{-1}\rho P\quad\allowbreak \text{and}\quad\allowbreak A'=P^{-1}AP.ρ′=P−1ρPandA′=P−1AP. Therefore tr⁡(ρ′A′)=tr⁡(P−1ρAP)=tr⁡(ρA).\tr(\rho'A')=\tr(P^{-1}\rho A P)=\tr(\rho A).tr(ρ′A′)=tr(P−1ρAP)=tr(ρA). Thus the expectation value ⟨A⟩=tr⁡(ρA)\expval{A}=\tr(\rho A)⟨A⟩=tr(ρA) does not depend on the chosen coordinates. It is a property of the physical quantity, not of the way we write it down. Lecture 10 gives the physical definitions and hypotheses; here we have only applied the change-of-basis formula.

Once bases of VVV and WWW are fixed, we can write each map T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W) as a matrix M(T)\Mat(T)M(T). Its columns are the images of the basis vectors, written in coordinates, and [Tv]C=M(T)[v]B[Tv]_{\mathcal C}=\Mat(T)[v]_{\mathcal B}[Tv]C​=M(T)[v]B​. Composition of maps corresponds to matrix multiplication; that is exactly why matrices are multiplied by the “row times column” rule. The space of matrices is isomorphic to the space of maps: L(V,W)≅Fm×n\Lin(V,W)\iso\F^{m\times n}L(V,W)≅Fm×n and dim⁡L(V,W)=(dim⁡V)(dim⁡W)\dim\Lin(V,W)=(\dim V)(\dim W)dimL(V,W)=(dimV)(dimW). A map is an isomorphism if and only if it is bijective. Finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic if and only if their dimensions agree, so every nnn-dimensional space is, in effect, a copy of Fn\F^nFn. Under a change of basis, an operator’s matrix AAA becomes the similar matrix P−1APP^{-1}APP−1AP. Trace and determinant are similarity invariants, so they are properties of the operator itself.

  • Matrix of a map—its columns are the images of the basis vectors, written in coordinates; the matrix depends on the basis.
  • Composition →\to→ multiplication—this is where the row-times-column rule comes from; L(V,W)≅Fm×n\Lin(V,W)\iso\F^{m\times n}L(V,W)≅Fm×n.
  • Isomorphism—a bijective linear map; finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic   ⟺  \iff⟺ their dimensions agree.
  • Change of basis / similarity—A↦P−1APA\mapsto P^{-1}APA↦P−1AP; trace and determinant are invariant, so they belong to the operator.
