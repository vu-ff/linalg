Lecture 4 ended with an important example: in a well-chosen basis the matrix of a reflection became diagonal, diag⁡(1,−1)\diag(1,-1)diag(1,−1). The diagonal entries were the factors by which the operator multiplied two special directions. In this lecture we give these objects names. Directions that an operator only multiplies by a number are called eigenvectors, and the factors themselves are called eigenvalues. We will learn to find them with the characteristic polynomial. We will prove that over C\CC an eigenvalue always exists. Finally, we will show that over C\CC there is always a basis in which the matrix of the operator is upper triangular, with the eigenvalues on its diagonal.

Unless an example explicitly says otherwise, VVV is a nonzero finite-dimensional vector space over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C} and T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V).

In quantum mechanics eigenvalues have a direct physical meaning. The equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ describes a state ψ\psiψ whose energy is definite and equal to EEE. So the energy levels are the eigenvalues of the operator H^\hat HH^. In infinite-dimensional spaces an operator can also have a continuous spectrum, and the lines of an atomic spectrum show not the energies themselves but their differences. We discuss these qualifications in Lectures 10 and 14.

Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

Guiding question.

Which parts of a vector space stay unchanged when an operator acts on them? Given T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V), we first look for the subspaces that TTT preserves.

Definition 5.1 (Invariant subspace). A subspace U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V is called invariant under the operator TTT if TTT maps every vector of UUU to a vector of the same subspace: u∈U⟹Tu∈U.u\in U \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Tu\in U.u∈U⟹Tu∈U. Equivalently, T(U)⊆U.T(U)\subseteq U.T(U)⊆U.

Familiar invariant subspaces.

The following subspaces are always invariant under TTT: V,{0},ker⁡T,im⁡T.V,\qquad \set0,\qquad \ker T,\qquad \img T.V,{0},kerT,imT. The smallest nontrivial invariant subspaces are lines. For v≠0v\neq0v=0, the line span⁡(v)\spn(v)span(v) is invariant exactly when the vector TvTvTv is a multiple of the vector vvv. We call such a line an eigenline.

Definition 5.2 (Eigenvalue, eigenvector, eigenspace). A nonzero vector stays on its line exactly when Tv=λv,v≠0.Tv=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq0.Tv=λv,v=0.

  • Eigenvalue. A scalar λ∈F\lambda\in\Fλ∈F for which this equation holds is called an eigenvalue of TTT. It is the factor by which TTT multiplies the vector.

  • Eigenvector. A nonzero vector vvv satisfying this equation is called an eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ. It is a direction that TTT preserves. An eigenvalue together with a corresponding eigenvector is called an eigenpair.

  • Eigenspace. All vectors with the same eigenvalue, together with the zero vector, form the eigenspace E(λ,T)=ker⁡(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv}.E(\lambda,T)=\ker(T-\lambda I)=\set{v\in V : Tv=\lambda v}.E(λ,T)=ker(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv}.

The set of eigenvalues is called the point spectrum. The spectrum spec⁡(T)\spec(T)spec(T) of an operator is the set of scalars λ\lambdaλ for which the operator T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI is not invertible. In a finite-dimensional space these two sets coincide (see (5.1) below). In an infinite-dimensional space this equality can fail; we discuss this in Lecture 14.

Three equivalent statements.

The eigenspace is a kernel, so for every scalar λ\lambdaλ the following statements are equivalent: λ∈spec⁡(T)  ⟺  E(λ,T)≠{0}  ⟺  T−λI is not injective  ⟺  T−λI is not invertible. \begin{aligned} \lambda\in\spec(T) &\iff E(\lambda,T)\neq\set0 \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not injective} \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible.} \end{aligned} λ∈spec(T)​⟺E(λ,T)={0}⟺T−λI is not injective⟺T−λI is not invertible.​ The second equivalence is the theorem “Injectivity is a kernel condition” of Lecture 3. The third rests on the corollary “Operators: injective === surjective” of Lecture 3: on a finite-dimensional space an operator is injective if and only if it is invertible. Geometrically, an eigenvector is a direction that TTT only multiplies by a number (Figure 1).

  • Projection. The eigenvalues of a projection, i.e. an operator satisfying P2=PP^2=PP2=P, lie in the set {0,1}\set{0,1}{0,1}, and its eigenspaces are E(1,P)=im⁡PE(1,P)=\img PE(1,P)=imP and E(0,P)=ker⁡PE(0,P)=\ker PE(0,P)=kerP.

Computing eigenvalues and eigenspaces

Guiding question.

How do we find eigenvalues? In coordinates, an eigenvalue shows up as a non-invertible matrix, and invertibility can be tested with the determinant.

Definition 5.6 (Characteristic polynomial). The characteristic polynomial of a square matrix A∈Mn×n(F)A\in M_{n\times n}(\F)A∈Mn×n​(F) is the polynomial χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A). The characteristic polynomial of an operator T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) is the characteristic polynomial of its matrix in any basis; we show below that it does not depend on the basis. The characteristic polynomial has degree nnn and leading coefficient 111. Its roots are exactly the eigenvalues of AAA: χA(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.\chi_A(\lambda)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A\text{.}χA​(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.

This follows from (5.1): the matrix λI−A\lambda I-AλI−A is not invertible if and only if its determinant is zero.

Why λI−A\lambda I-AλI−A and not A−λIA-\lambda IA−λI.

In Example 5.4 we solved the equation det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A-\lambda I)=0det(A−λI)=0. The matrices λI−A\lambda I-AλI−A and A−λIA-\lambda IA−λI differ only in sign, i.e. each of the nnn rows is multiplied by −1-1−1, so det⁡(λI−A)=(−1)ndet⁡(A−λI).\det(\lambda I-A)=(-1)^n\det(A-\lambda I).det(λI−A)=(−1)ndet(A−λI). The two determinants have the same roots, and in the definition we choose λI−A\lambda I-AλI−A so that the leading coefficient is 111. The two matrices also have the same kernel, so we keep computing eigenspaces as ker⁡(A−λI)\ker(A-\lambda I)ker(A−λI).

Remark 5.7 (Determinant properties we will need). We will use four properties of the determinant, which we state without proof.

  • Invertibility. A square matrix is invertible if and only if det⁡A≠0\det A\neq0detA=0.

  • Product. det⁡(AB)=det⁡A det⁡B\det(AB)=\det A\,\det Bdet(AB)=detAdetB. Hence det⁡(P−1AP)=det⁡A.\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det A.det(P−1AP)=detA.

  • Triangular matrix. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.

  • Coefficients. det⁡(λI−A)=λn−(tr⁡A)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndet⁡A.\det(\lambda I-A) =\lambda^n-(\tr A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A.det(λI−A)=λn−(trA)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndetA.

All of them follow from expanding the determinant along a row or a column, or from row operations. The proofs can be found in the course literature.

Why the characteristic polynomial does not depend on the basis.

The matrices of the same operator in different bases are related by a similarity transformation (Lecture 4). Therefore det⁡(λI−P−1AP)=det⁡(P−1(λI−A)P)=det⁡(λI−A),\det(\lambda I-P^{-1}AP)=\det\bigl(P^{-1}(\lambda I-A)P\bigr)=\det(\lambda I-A),det(λI−P−1AP)=det(P−1(λI−A)P)=det(λI−A), so χP−1AP=χA.\chi_{P^{-1}AP}=\chi_A.χP−1AP​=χA​. Hence the characteristic polynomial and its roots, the eigenvalues, do not depend on the chosen basis.

Proposition 5.8 (Trace and determinant from eigenvalues). Comparing the coefficients of λn−1\lambda^{n-1}λn−1 and the constant terms gives both identities. 

The method for finding eigenpairs.

For a concrete matrix AAA we find the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors separately.

  1. Form the characteristic polynomial, χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A).

  2. Find the eigenvalues by solving the equation χA(λ)=0\chi_A(\lambda)=0χA​(λ)=0.

  3. Check the result: compare the sum and the product of the eigenvalues with tr⁡A\tr AtrA and det⁡A\det AdetA (Proposition 5.8).

  4. For each eigenvalue, find the eigenspace E(λ,A)=ker⁡(A−λI)E(\lambda,A)=\ker(A-\lambda I)E(λ,A)=ker(A−λI) and a basis of it.

Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent

Guiding question.

When are several eigenvectors linearly independent, so that they can become part of a basis? It is enough for their eigenvalues to be distinct.

What this theorem gives.

Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are always linearly independent. There is no need to check their independence by Gaussian elimination.

Corollary 5.14 (Number of eigenvalues). An operator on an nnn-dimensional space has at most nnn distinct eigenvalues.

Proof. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues form a linearly independent list. By the theorem “Independent ≤\le≤ spanning” of Lecture 2, the length of such a list is at most n=dim⁡Vn=\dim Vn=dimV. 

Corollary 5.15 (The sum of eigenspaces is direct). If λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​ are distinct eigenvalues of TTT, then E(λ1,T)+⋯+E(λm,T)=E(λ1,T)⊕⋯⊕E(λm,T).E(\lambda_1,T)+\cdots+E(\lambda_m,T) =E(\lambda_1,T)\dsum\cdots\dsum E(\lambda_m,T).E(λ1​,T)+⋯+E(λm​,T)=E(λ1​,T)⊕⋯⊕E(λm​,T).

Proof. Suppose we have written the zero vector as a sum 0=u1+⋯+um,where uj∈E(λj,T) and not all uj=0.0 = u_1+\cdots+u_m, \quad\text{where $u_j\in E(\lambda_j,T)$ and not all $u_j=0$.}0=u1​+⋯+um​,where uj​∈E(λj​,T) and not all uj​=0. The nonzero terms are eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues, and their sum is zero. This contradicts Theorem 5.13. So the zero vector can be written only trivially, and the sum is direct. 

Connection with diagonalization.

Theorem 5.13 gives the following chain of implications when dim⁡V=n\dim V=ndimV=n: n distinct eigenvalues⟹n independent eigenvectors⟹a basis of eigenvectors.\begin{aligned} n\text{ distinct eigenvalues} &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\text{ independent eigenvectors}\\ &\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{a basis of eigenvectors}. \end{aligned}n distinct eigenvalues​⟹n independent eigenvectors⟹a basis of eigenvectors.​ In a basis of eigenvectors the matrix of the operator is diagonal. We call an operator diagonalizable if it has a basis of eigenvectors. We derive a general criterion for diagonalizability in Lecture 6.

Existence of eigenvalues over C\CC

Guiding question.

Why can a real rotation have no eigenvector, while a complex operator is guaranteed to have one? It all depends on whether the characteristic polynomial must have a root.

Theorem 5.16 (Eigenvalues exist over C\CC). If V≠{0},dim⁡V<∞,V is a complex vector space,V\neq\set0,\qquad \dim V<\infty,\qquad V\text{ is a complex vector space},V={0},dimV<∞,V is a complex vector space, then every T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) has an eigenvalue.

Proof. Choose a basis of VVV and let AAA be the matrix of TTT in it. We rely on the fundamental theorem of algebra: every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root. We do not prove this theorem, because its proof rests on analysis, not on linear algebra. The proof is the following chain of implications: deg⁡χA=dim⁡V≥1⇒fundamental theorem of algebraχA(λ)=0 for some λ∈C⟹det⁡(λI−A)=0⟹ker⁡(λI−A)≠{0}⟹Av=λv for some v≠0.\begin{aligned} \deg\chi_A=\dim V\ge1 &\xRightarrow{\text{fundamental theorem of algebra}} \chi_A(\lambda)=0\text{ for some }\lambda\in\C \\ &\Longrightarrow \det(\lambda I-A)=0 \\ &\Longrightarrow \ker(\lambda I-A)\neq\set0 \\ &\Longrightarrow Av=\lambda v\text{ for some }v\neq0. \end{aligned}degχA​=dimV≥1​fundamental theorem of algebra​χA​(λ)=0 for some λ∈C⟹det(λI−A)=0⟹ker(λI−A)={0}⟹Av=λv for some v=0.​ So λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue of TTT. 

Remark 5.17.

  • Where C\CC is needed. The fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees that a root exists. A real polynomial need not have a real root.

  • What the theorem does not say. In quantum mechanics complex numbers describe phase and interference. But the existence of eigenvalues alone does not yet explain measurement. Which operators correspond to measurable quantities, and with what probability each outcome is obtained, we discuss in Lectures 9–10.

Multiplicity and defective operators

Guiding question.

Does an eigenvalue repeated kkk times always give kkk linearly independent eigenvectors?

Two multiplicities.

For each eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ we define two numbers:

  • The algebraic multiplicity mult⁡χT(λ)\operatorname{mult}_{\chi_T}(\lambda)multχT​​(λ) is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ as a root of the polynomial χT\chi_TχT​.

  • The geometric multiplicity is the dimension of the eigenspace dim⁡E(λ,T)=dim⁡ker⁡(T−λI).\dim E(\lambda,T)=\dim\ker(T-\lambda I).dimE(λ,T)=dimker(T−λI).

Example 5.11 shows that these numbers can differ.

Proposition 5.18 (Geometric ≤\le≤ algebraic). For every eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, dim⁡E(λ,T)≤mult⁡χT(λ).\dim E(\lambda,T) \le \operatorname{mult}_{\chi_T}(\lambda).dimE(λ,T)≤multχT​​(λ).

Proof (self-study). Let g=dim⁡E(λ,T)g=\dim E(\lambda,T)g=dimE(λ,T).

  1. Let v1,…,vgv_1,\dots,v_gv1​,…,vg​ be a basis of E(λ,T)E(\lambda,T)E(λ,T). Extend it to a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ of VVV.

  2. Since Tvk=λvkTv_k=\lambda v_kTvk​=λvk​ for k≤gk\le gk≤g, the matrix of TTT in this basis has the block form M(T)=(λIgB0C).\Mat(T)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda I_g & B\\[2pt]0&C\end{pmatrix}.M(T)=(λIg​0​BC​).

  3. This matrix is block upper triangular, so its determinant is the product of the determinants of the diagonal blocks. This can be seen by expanding the determinant along the first column: because of the zero block, only one term survives at each step. So the characteristic polynomial factors as χT(t)=det⁡(tIg−λIg)det⁡(tIn−g−C)=(t−λ)gdet⁡(tIn−g−C).\chi_T(t) =\det\bigl(tI_g-\lambda I_g\bigr)\det\bigl(tI_{n-g}-C\bigr) =(t-\lambda)^g\det\bigl(tI_{n-g}-C\bigr).χT​(t)=det(tIg​−λIg​)det(tIn−g​−C)=(t−λ)gdet(tIn−g​−C).

  4. Hence the polynomial χT(t)\chi_T(t)χT​(t) is divisible by (t−λ)g(t-\lambda)^g(t−λ)g. So the multiplicity of λ\lambdaλ as a root is at least ggg.

 

When diagonalization fails.

An operator is called defective if the geometric multiplicity of at least one of its eigenvalues is smaller than the algebraic one. The standard example is the shear S=(1101).S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.S=(10​11​). Its only eigenvalue 111 has the multiplicities mult⁡χS(1)⏟2  >  dim⁡E(1,S)⏟1,E(1,S)=span⁡((1,0)).\underbrace{\operatorname{mult}_{\chi_S}(1)}_{2} \;>\; \underbrace{\dim E(1,S)}_{1}, \qquad E(1,S)=\spn\bigl((1,0)\bigr).2multχS​​(1)​​>1dimE(1,S)​​,E(1,S)=span((1,0)). So the shear does not have enough eigenvectors to form a basis.

Upper triangular matrices

Guiding question.

What can we do when an operator does not have enough eigenvectors to form a basis? Over C\CC it is still possible to find a basis in which the matrix of the operator is upper triangular.

Invariant flag.

An ordered basis defines nested subspaces span⁡(v1)⊆span⁡(v1,v2)⊆⋯⊆span⁡(v1,…,vn)=V.\spn(v_1) \subseteq\spn(v_1,v_2) \subseteq\cdots\subseteq \spn(v_1,\dots,v_n)=V.span(v1​)⊆span(v1​,v2​)⊆⋯⊆span(v1​,…,vn​)=V. Such a chain of subspaces is called a flag. The matrix is upper triangular exactly when the operator TTT leaves every subspace of this flag invariant.

Proposition 5.19 (Triangular matrices and invariant subspaces [self-study]). Let v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ be a basis of VVV. The following statements are equivalent: M(T,(v1,…,vn)) is upper triangular⟺Tvk∈span⁡(v1,…,vk)(k=1,…,n)⟺span⁡(v1,…,vk) is invariant for every k.\begin{align*} & \Mat(T, (v_1,\dots,v_n) )\text{ is upper triangular} \\ &\quad \Longleftrightarrow\quad Tv_k\in\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)\quad(k=1,\dots,n) \\ &\quad \Longleftrightarrow\quad \spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)\text{ is invariant for every }k. \end{align*}​M(T,(v1​,…,vn​)) is upper triangular⟺Tvk​∈span(v1​,…,vk​)(k=1,…,n)⟺span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant for every k.​

Proof. The kkk-th column of the matrix contains the coordinates of the vector TvkTv_kTvk​. The entries of this column below the kkk-th row are zero exactly when Tvk∈span⁡(v1,…,vk).Tv_k\in\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k).Tvk​∈span(v1​,…,vk​). This is the first equivalence. If the subspace span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant, then the vector TvkTv_kTvk​ belongs to it. Conversely, if the second condition holds for all kkk, then for every j≤kj\le kj≤k the vector TvjTv_jTvj​ belongs to the subspace span⁡(v1,…,vj)⊆span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(v_1,\dots,v_j)\subseteq\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(v1​,…,vj​)⊆span(v1​,…,vk​). So the subspace span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant.

For every operator TTT on a finite-dimensional complex vector space there is a basis in which M(T)=(λ1∗⋯∗0λ2⋯∗⋮⋱⋱⋮0⋯0λn).\Mat(T)= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1&*&\cdots&*\\ 0&\lambda_2&\cdots&*\\ \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&\cdots&0&\lambda_n \end{pmatrix}.M(T)=​λ1​0⋮0​∗λ2​⋱⋯​⋯⋯⋱0​∗∗⋮λn​​​.

Proof sketch (self-study). We use induction on n=dim⁡Vn=\dim Vn=dimV.

  1. By Theorem 5.16, the operator TTT has an eigenvector v1v_1v1​. Its line span⁡(v1)\spn(v_1)span(v1​) is invariant.

  2. Consider the (n−1)(n-1)(n−1)-dimensional quotient space V/span⁡(v1)V/\spn(v_1)V/span(v1​) (Lecture 3). The line span⁡(v1)\spn(v_1)span(v1​) is invariant, so the formula Tˉ(v+span⁡(v1))=Tv+span⁡(v1)\bar T\bigl(v+\spn(v_1)\bigr)=Tv+\spn(v_1)Tˉ(v+span(v1​))=Tv+span(v1​) gives a well-defined operator on this quotient space.

  4. Choose a representative of each coset; this gives vectors v2,…,vn∈Vv_2,\dots,v_n\in Vv2​,…,vn​∈V. Together with v1v_1v1​ they form a basis of VVV that satisfies the condition Tvk∈span⁡(v1,…,vk).Tv_k\in\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k).Tvk​∈span(v1​,…,vk​).

Complex numbers are needed only to find an eigenvalue at each step. So the same proof works over any field in which the polynomial χT\chi_TχT​ splits into linear factors. 

Eigenvalues on the diagonal.

The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix can be read directly off its diagonal.

Theorem 5.21 (Eigenvalues sit on the diagonal). If TTT has an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries λ1,…,λn\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_nλ1​,…,λn​, then spec⁡(T)={λ1,…,λn}.\spec(T)=\set{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n}.spec(T)={λ1​,…,λn​}. Moreover, T is invertible⟺λk≠0 for every k.T\text{ is invertible} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda_k\neq0\text{ for every }k.T is invertible⟺λk​=0 for every k.

Proof. Let AAA be the upper triangular matrix of TTT. Then the matrix λI−A\lambda I-AλI−A is also upper triangular, so by Remark 5.7 χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)=(λ−λ1)⋯(λ−λn).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda-\lambda_1)\cdots(\lambda-\lambda_n).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A)=(λ−λ1​)⋯(λ−λn​). The roots of this polynomial are exactly the diagonal entries. Also, det⁡A=λ1⋯λn,\det A=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n,detA=λ1​⋯λn​, and this product is nonzero exactly when every diagonal entry is nonzero. A matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero.

Connection to physics: observables

In the last example we will see what eigenvalues mean in quantum mechanics.

Example 5.23 (The Pauli matrices σx\sigma_xσx​ and σy\sigma_yσy​). The spin projections onto the xxx and yyy axes, measured in units of ℏ/2\hbar/2ℏ/2, are described by the Pauli matrices σx=(0110) and σy=(0−ii0).\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{ and }\quad \sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}.σx​=(01​10​) and σy​=(0i​−i0​). Both matrices have the characteristic polynomial χ(λ)=λ2−1\chi(\lambda)=\lambda^2-1χ(λ)=λ2−1, and their eigenvalues are +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. Physical meaning.

When the spin projection onto the xxx axis is measured, only two outcomes are possible, +1+1+1 and −1-1−1. These are the eigenvalues of the matrix σx\sigma_xσx​. The corresponding eigenvectors v±v_\pmv±​ describe the states in which the outcome of the measurement is known in advance. The vectors w±w_\pmw±​ describe spin states pointing along the +y+y+y and −y-y−y axes. The eigenvectors w±w_\pmw±​ of the matrix σy\sigma_yσy​ also have an imaginary coordinate, so the space of quantum states is naturally complex. We discuss the probabilities of measurement outcomes in Lecture 10.

In an eigenpair, the eigenvalue is a factor and the eigenvector is a direction: Tv=λv,E(λ,T)=ker⁡(T−λI).Tv=\lambda v, \qquad E(\lambda,T)=\ker(T-\lambda I).Tv=λv,E(λ,T)=ker(T−λI). In finite dimension, λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue exactly when T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI is not invertible.

The roots of the characteristic polynomial are the eigenvalues, and we find the eigenvectors by computing kernels with Gaussian elimination. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. If an operator on an nnn-dimensional space has nnn distinct eigenvalues, then there is a basis of eigenvectors.

Over C\CC the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees at least one eigenvalue. Repeating this argument with invariant subspaces, we get a basis in which the matrix of the operator is upper triangular, with all the eigenvalues on its diagonal.

  • Eigenvalue, eigenvector, eigenspace—Tv=λvTv=\lambda vTv=λv; E(λ,T)=ker⁡(T−λI)E(\lambda,T)=\ker(T-\lambda I)E(λ,T)=ker(T−λI); in finite dimension, λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue   ⟺  T−λI\iff T-\lambda I⟺T−λI is not invertible.
  • Independence of eigenvectors—eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent; there are at most dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues.
  • Eigenvalues exist over C\CC—every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional complex space has an eigenvalue (fundamental theorem of algebra).
  • Characteristic polynomial—χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A)\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A)χA​(λ)=det(λI−A); its roots are the eigenvalues, and tr⁡A=∑λk\tr A=\sum\lambda_ktrA=∑λk​, det⁡A=∏λk\det A=\prod\lambda_kdetA=∏λk​.
  • Triangularizability over C\CC—in a suitable basis the matrix of a complex operator is upper triangular, with the eigenvalues on its diagonal.
