So far our vector spaces have had no geometry: no length, no angle, no notion of two vectors being perpendicular. The inner product supplies all three. You know it on R2\R^2R2 and R3\R^3R3; now we define it on any vector space, including a complex one. In quantum mechanics the squared modulus of the inner product of two unit state vectors is a probability, and the main result of the lecture, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, shows that it never exceeds 111. Lectures 7–10 work with finite-dimensional spaces over R\RR or C\CC; function spaces appear only as examples.

We use the physics convention: the inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot and linear in the second. Many mathematics texts put the conjugation in the second slot; their formulas convert by swapping the two arguments. At the end of the lecture we translate everything into Dirac’s bra–ket notation.

Inner products

Definition 7.1 (Inner product, physics convention). An inner product on a complex vector space VVV assigns to each ordered pair u,v∈Vu,v\in Vu,v∈V a scalar ⟨u,v⟩∈C\inner{u}{v}\in\C⟨u,v⟩∈C such that

  1. conjugate symmetry: ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩‾\inner{u}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{u}}⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩​;

  2. linearity in the second slot: ⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩\inner{u}{av+bw}=a\inner{u}{v}+b\inner{u}{w}⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩;

  3. positive definiteness: ⟨v,v⟩≥0\inner{v}{v}\ge0⟨v,v⟩≥0, with equality iff v=0v=0v=0.

A vector space with an inner product is an inner product space.

By conjugate symmetry ⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩‾\inner{v}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{v}}⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩​, so the number ⟨v,v⟩\inner{v}{v}⟨v,v⟩ is real. Hence the positive-definiteness condition makes sense. This is why the first slot carries a conjugation: without it ⟨v,v⟩\inner vv⟨v,v⟩ could be complex, and we could not define the length of a vector. Combining conjugate symmetry with linearity in the second slot gives conjugate-linearity in the first slot: ⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩‾=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩‾=a‾ ⟨u,v⟩+b‾ ⟨w,v⟩.\inner{au+bw}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{au+bw}} =\conj{a\inner{v}{u}+b\inner{v}{w}} =\conj a\,\inner{u}{v}+\conj b\,\inner{w}{v}.⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩​=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩​=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨w,v⟩. So scalars come out of the first slot conjugated and out of the second slot unchanged. Such a product is “one and a half” times linear, and is called sesquilinear. Over the reals the conjugation changes nothing. So a real inner product is a symmetric, positive-definite bilinear form, that is, one that is linear in each slot separately. Bilinear forms are studied in their own right in Lecture 11.

Dirac notation, amplitudes, and what comes next

Let us now translate the ideas of this lecture into the language of quantum mechanics. Physicists write vectors as ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ and call them kets. To each vector ϕ\phiϕ Dirac assigns a bra ⟨ϕ∣\bra{\phi}⟨ϕ∣. It is the linear map that sends each ket ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ to a number, the inner product: ⟨ϕ∣ ⁣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ | ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩.\bra{\phi}\colon\ket{\psi}\longmapsto\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\inner{\phi}{\psi}.⟨ϕ∣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩. A linear map from a space VVV to the scalars is called a linear functional. Linear functionals are studied in more detail in Lecture 8.

The inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, so the correspondence ∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣\ket{\phi}\mapsto\bra{\phi}∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣ is conjugate-linear too: a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a‾ ⟨ϕ∣+b‾ ⟨χ∣.a\ket{\phi}+b\ket{\chi}\mapsto\conj a\,\bra{\phi}+\conj b\,\bra{\chi}.a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a⟨ϕ∣+b⟨χ∣. So the bracket ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ and conjugate-linear in the vector ϕ\phiϕ that labels the bra, exactly as Definition 7.1 requires.

Remark 7.24 (The Born rule). In quantum mechanics the state of a system is described by a unit vector ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩, that is, ⟨ψ | ψ⟩=1\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1. Let ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩ be another such state. The number ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is called a probability amplitude. By the Born rule, the square of its modulus, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2, is the probability that a measurement finds the system, prepared in the state ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩, in the state ∣ϕ⟩\ket{\phi}∣ϕ⟩. You will study this rule in detail in the Quantum Mechanics course.

Remark 7.25 (Amplitudes are bounded). When ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩ and ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ are unit vectors, Cauchy–Schwarz (Theorem 7.16) gives ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣≤1.\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}\le1 .∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣≤1. So the modulus of an amplitude never exceeds one. Hence the Born-rule number ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 lies between zero and one, as a probability must. In Lecture 9 the same inequality will give the Heisenberg uncertainty relation.

In finite dimensions the answer is yes: the Gram–Schmidt process constructs one. In an orthonormal basis the inner product becomes the familiar sum ∑kuk‾vk\sum_k\conj{u_k}v_k∑k​uk​​vk​, and the matrices of operators become especially simple.

An inner product is conjugate symmetric, positive definite, and linear in the second slot. Hence it is conjugate-linear in the first slot, that is, sesquilinear (the physics convention). The inner product defines the norm ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm v=\sqrt{\inner v v}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​ and orthogonality ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner u v=0⟨u,v⟩=0, and the Pythagorean theorem links them. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥\abs{\inner u v}\le\norm u\norm v∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥ follows from the orthogonal decomposition and the Pythagorean theorem. The triangle inequality follows from it. The parallelogram law and the polarization identity show that the inner product is determined by the lengths of vectors. In Dirac notation ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ and conjugate-linear in ϕ\phiϕ. In quantum mechanics states are unit vectors, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket\phi\psi}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is a probability, and a suitable measurement tells orthogonal states apart. These ideas are needed for the spectral theorem in Lecture 10.

  • Inner product, physics convention — conjugate symmetric, linear in the second slot, positive definite; ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket.
  • Norm and orthogonality — ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm v=\sqrt{\inner v v}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​; ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner u v=0⟨u,v⟩=0; the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Cauchy–Schwarz inequality — ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥ ∥v∥\abs{\inner u v}\le\norm u\,\norm v∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥; the modulus of an amplitude is at most one.
  • Triangle inequality, parallelogram law, and polarization — properties of lengths and distances; ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩ is recovered from ∥⋅∥\norm{\cdot}∥⋅∥.
  • Physical picture — states are unit vectors, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is a probability, orthogonality means distinguishability.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A scalar leaves the first slot conjugated and the second slot unchanged. The first slot is conjugate-linear, so 2−i comes out as 2+i; the second slot is linear, so i comes out as i. Hence ⟨(2−i)u,iv⟩=(2+i)i⟨u,v⟩=(−1+2i)⟨u,v⟩. 7 Vectors v₁, v₂ have the Gram matrix G=[[2,1+2i],[1−2i,c]]. For which c are v₁ and v₂ linearly dependent?

The vectors are linearly dependent exactly when G is not invertible, that is, when det G=0.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. det G=2c−(1+2i)(1−2i)=2c−5, which is 0 for c=5/2. 8 In ℂ³ with the standard inner product, U=span((1,i,0),(0,1,1)). Which vector lies in U⊥?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A vector lies in U⊥ when it is orthogonal to both vectors that span U. Conjugate the coordinates in the first slot. ⟨(1,i,0),(i,1,−1)⟩=i+(−i)·1=0 and ⟨(0,1,1),(i,1,−1)⟩=1−1=0. So (i,1,−1) is orthogonal to both spanning vectors, and by conjugate-linearity in the first slot to all of U. (−i,1,−1) passes the first test only if i is not conjugated. (i,1,0) is orthogonal to (1,i,0) but not to (0,1,1), and (0,1,−1) is orthogonal to (0,1,1) but not to (1,i,0).

12 |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthonormal, and |+⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. Which unit vector describes the same physical state as |+⟩?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Only a common factor eⁱᶿ on the whole vector leaves the state unchanged. −(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 is (−1)|+⟩, and −1=eⁱᶿ with θ=π is a phase, so no probability changes.
