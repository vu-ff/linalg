Lecture 7

Inner Product Spaces

By the end of this lecture you will be able to

  • Verify the inner-product axioms in the physics convention (conjugate-linear first slot).
  • Translate between the notation ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩ and Dirac’s notation ⟨⋅ | ⋅⟩\braket{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅∣⋅⟩.
  • Compute norms, check orthogonality, and apply the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Prove and apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the triangle inequality.
  • Recover the inner product from the norm by the polarization identity.

So far our vector spaces have had no geometry: no length, no angle, no notion of two vectors being perpendicular. The inner product supplies all three at once. You already know it on R2\R^2R2 and R3\R^3R3 from your mathematics course. Now we define it on any vector space, including a complex one.

The complex inner product is the foundation of quantum mechanics. In the Quantum Mechanics course you will learn that the state of a physical system is described by a complex vector, and that the inner product of two states gives the probability that a system in one state will be found in the other. We write the inner product in the physics convention and in the bra–ket notation physicists use, due to Dirac. The main result of the lecture, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, will show that such a probability never exceeds 111.

A word on convention. We take the inner product to be conjugate-linear in the first slot and linear in the second. This is the usual choice in physics: it makes ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ linear in the second vector, the ket ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩. Many mathematics texts put the conjugation in the second slot; their formulas convert by swapping the two arguments.

Remark 7.1 (What Lectures 7–10 cover). This lecture and the next three work only with finite-dimensional spaces over R\RR or C\CC. The physics examples use complex spaces, because quantum states are described by complex vectors. A vector and any nonzero multiple of it describe the same physical state. So we usually describe states by unit vectors (of length 111). Even then, the unit vectors vvv and eiθve^{i\theta}veiθv, with θ∈R\theta\in\Rθ∈R, describe the same state.

Inner products

Definition 7.2 (Inner product, physics convention). An inner product on a complex vector space VVV assigns to each ordered pair u,v∈Vu,v\in Vu,v∈V a scalar ⟨u,v⟩∈C\inner{u}{v}\in\C⟨u,v⟩∈C such that

  1. conjugate symmetry: ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩‾\inner{u}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{u}}⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩​;

  2. linearity in the second slot: ⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩\inner{u}{av+bw}=a\inner{u}{v}+b\inner{u}{w}⟨u,av+bw⟩=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨u,w⟩;

  3. positive definiteness: ⟨v,v⟩≥0\inner{v}{v}\ge0⟨v,v⟩≥0, with equality iff v=0v=0v=0.

A vector space with an inner product is an inner product space.

By conjugate symmetry ⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩‾\inner{v}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{v}}⟨v,v⟩=⟨v,v⟩​, so the number ⟨v,v⟩\inner{v}{v}⟨v,v⟩ is real. Hence the positive-definiteness condition makes sense. Combining conjugate symmetry with linearity in the second slot gives conjugate-linearity in the first slot: ⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩‾=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩‾=a‾ ⟨u,v⟩+b‾ ⟨w,v⟩.\inner{au+bw}{v}=\conj{\inner{v}{au+bw}} =\conj{a\inner{v}{u}+b\inner{v}{w}} =\conj a\,\inner{u}{v}+\conj b\,\inner{w}{v}.⟨au+bw,v⟩=⟨v,au+bw⟩​=a⟨v,u⟩+b⟨v,w⟩​=a⟨u,v⟩+b⟨w,v⟩. So scalars come out of the first slot conjugated and out of the second slot unchanged. Such a product is “one and a half” times linear, and is called sesquilinear. Over the reals the conjugation changes nothing. So a real inner product is a symmetric, positive-definite bilinear form, that is, one that is linear in each slot separately. Bilinear forms are studied in their own right in Lecture 11.

On Rn\R^nRn the conjugation changes nothing, and we get the familiar dot product ∑kukvk\sum_k u_kv_k∑k​uk​vk​. On the space of continuous functions on [a,b][a,b][a,b], ⟨f,g⟩=∫abf(t)‾ g(t) dt\inner{f}{g}=\int_a^b\conj{f(t)}\,g(t)\,dt⟨f,g⟩=∫ab​f(t)​g(t)dt is an inner product. In quantum mechanics, the states of particles are described in similar function spaces.

Remark 7.9 (Why the conjugation is needed). Why do we need the conjugation in the first slot? Without it the number ⟨v,v⟩\inner v v⟨v,v⟩ could be complex, and the condition ⟨v,v⟩≥0\inner v v\ge0⟨v,v⟩≥0 would make no sense. We could then not define the length of a vector. Conjugating the first slot keeps the product linear in the second slot and at the same time makes ⟨v,v⟩\inner v v⟨v,v⟩ real and nonnegative.

Dirac notation

Physicists write vectors as ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ and call them kets. To each vector ϕ\phiϕ Dirac assigns a bra ⟨ϕ∣\bra{\phi}⟨ϕ∣. It is the linear map that sends each ket ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩ to a number, the inner product: ⟨ϕ∣ ⁣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ | ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩.\bra{\phi}\colon\ket{\psi}\longmapsto\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\inner{\phi}{\psi}.⟨ϕ∣:∣ψ⟩⟼⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=⟨ϕ,ψ⟩. A linear map from a space VVV to the scalars is called a linear functional. Linear functionals are studied in more detail in Lecture 8.

The inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, so the correspondence ∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣\ket{\phi}\mapsto\bra{\phi}∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣ is conjugate-linear too: a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a‾ ⟨ϕ∣+b‾ ⟨χ∣.a\ket{\phi}+b\ket{\chi}\mapsto\conj a\,\bra{\phi}+\conj b\,\bra{\chi}.a∣ϕ⟩+b∣χ⟩↦a⟨ϕ∣+b⟨χ∣. So the bracket ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket and conjugate-linear in the bra, exactly as Definition 7.2 requires.

The product in the opposite order, ∣ψ⟩ ⁣⟨ϕ∣\ketbra{\psi}{\phi}∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ∣, is no longer a number but an operator. It sends a vector ∣χ⟩\ket{\chi}∣χ⟩ to the vector ∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ | χ⟩,\ket{\psi}\braket{\phi}{\chi},∣ψ⟩⟨ϕ∣χ⟩, that is, to a multiple of ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩. Such an operator is called an outer product. Its rank is one, because all its values are multiples of ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩. In Lecture 8 we will build projections and resolutions of the identity from outer products.

Remark 7.10 (The Born rule). In quantum mechanics the state of a system is described by a vector ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ with ⟨ψ | ψ⟩=1\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1. Let ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩ be another such state. The number ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is called a probability amplitude. By the Born rule, the square of its modulus, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2, is the probability that a measurement finds the system, prepared in the state ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩, in the state ∣ϕ⟩\ket{\phi}∣ϕ⟩. You will study this rule in detail in the Quantum Mechanics course. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality will show that this probability never exceeds 111.

Norm, orthogonality, and the Pythagorean theorem

Definition 7.15 (Norm and orthogonality). The norm of a vector vvv is ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩.\norm{v}=\sqrt{\inner{v}{v}}.∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​. A vector with ∥v∥=1\norm{v}=1∥v∥=1 is a unit vector (in physics, a normalized state). Two vectors are orthogonal, u⊥vu\perp vu⊥v, if ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner{u}{v}=0⟨u,v⟩=0. Vectors e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​ are orthonormal if they are unit vectors and pairwise orthogonal, that is, ⟨ei,ej⟩=δij,\inner{e_i}{e_j}=\delta_{ij},⟨ei​,ej​⟩=δij​, where δij=1\delta_{ij}=1δij​=1 if i=ji=ji=j and δij=0\delta_{ij}=0δij​=0 if i≠ji\neq ji=j (the Kronecker delta). Orthonormal bases are studied in detail in Lecture 8.

The norm is homogeneous: for every scalar aaa, ∥av∥=⟨av,av⟩=a‾ a ⟨v,v⟩=∣a∣ ∥v∥.\norm{av}=\sqrt{\inner{av}{av}}=\sqrt{\conj a\,a\,\inner{v}{v}}=\abs a\,\norm v .∥av∥=⟨av,av⟩​=aa⟨v,v⟩​=∣a∣∥v∥. Moreover, by positive definiteness the norm is zero only when v=0v=0v=0. The number ∥u−v∥\norm{u-v}∥u−v∥ is called the distance between the vectors uuu and vvv. Distances and convergence are studied in Lectures 13–14.

The norm and orthogonality are linked by the Pythagorean theorem.

Theorem 7.16 (Pythagorean theorem). If u⊥vu\perp vu⊥v, then ∥u+v∥2=∥u∥2+∥v∥2.\norm{u+v}^2=\norm u^2+\norm v^2 .∥u+v∥2=∥u∥2+∥v∥2.

Proof. Expanding the inner product in both slots gives ∥u+v∥2=⟨u+v,u+v⟩=∥u∥2+⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩+∥v∥2.\norm{u+v}^2=\inner{u+v}{u+v} =\norm u^2+\inner{u}{v}+\inner{v}{u}+\norm v^2 .∥u+v∥2=⟨u+v,u+v⟩=∥u∥2+⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩+∥v∥2. When u⊥vu\perp vu⊥v, both cross terms ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩=0\inner{u}{v}=\inner{v}{u}=0⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩=0 vanish, and ∥u∥2+∥v∥2\norm u^2+\norm v^2∥u∥2+∥v∥2 remains.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

Every vector uuu can be split into two parts: one parallel to a given vector vvv, the other orthogonal to it (Figure 1). This splitting, together with the Pythagorean theorem, proves the main inequality of this lecture.

Theorem 7.24 (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). In an inner product space, for all u,vu,vu,v, ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥ ∥v∥.\abs{\inner{u}{v}}\le\norm u\,\norm v .∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥. Equality holds if and only if u,vu,vu,v are linearly dependent.

Proof. If v=0v=0v=0, both sides of the inequality are zero, and the vectors uuu and 000 are linearly dependent. Assume from now on that v≠0v\neq0v=0, and write u=⟨v,u⟩∥v∥2 v+w,w=u−⟨v,u⟩∥v∥2 v.u=\frac{\inner{v}{u}}{\norm v^2}\,v+w,\qquad\allowbreak w=u-\frac{\inner{v}{u}}{\norm v^2}\,v .u=∥v∥2⟨v,u⟩​v+w,w=u−∥v∥2⟨v,u⟩​v. A direct check shows that w⊥vw\perp vw⊥v (Figure 1): ⟨v,w⟩=⟨v,u⟩−⟨v,u⟩∥v∥2⟨v,v⟩=0.\inner{v}{w}=\inner{v}{u}-\frac{\inner{v}{u}}{\norm v^2}\inner{v}{v}=0 .⟨v,w⟩=⟨v,u⟩−∥v∥2⟨v,u⟩​⟨v,v⟩=0. So the two summands are orthogonal, and by the Pythagorean theorem ∥u∥2=∣⟨v,u⟩∣2∥v∥2+∥w∥2≥∣⟨v,u⟩∣2∥v∥2.\norm u^2=\frac{\abs{\inner{v}{u}}^2}{\norm v^2}+\norm w^2 \ge\frac{\abs{\inner{v}{u}}^2}{\norm v^2}.∥u∥2=∥v∥2∣⟨v,u⟩∣2​+∥w∥2≥∥v∥2∣⟨v,u⟩∣2​. Multiplying by ∥v∥2\norm v^2∥v∥2 and taking square roots gives ∣⟨v,u⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥\abs{\inner{v}{u}}\le\norm u\norm v∣⟨v,u⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥. Triangle inequality, parallelogram law, and polarization

Theorem 7.30 (Triangle inequality). In an inner product space, for all u,vu,vu,v, ∥u+v∥≤∥u∥+∥v∥.\norm{u+v}\le\norm u+\norm v .∥u+v∥≤∥u∥+∥v∥.

Proof. As in the proof of the Pythagorean theorem, expand ∥u+v∥2\norm{u+v}^2∥u+v∥2. The cross terms add up to ⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩=2Re⁡⟨u,v⟩\inner{u}{v}+\inner{v}{u}=2\operatorname{Re}\inner{u}{v}⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩=2Re⟨u,v⟩, because ⟨v,u⟩=⟨u,v⟩‾\inner{v}{u}=\conj{\inner{u}{v}}⟨v,u⟩=⟨u,v⟩​. Next we use Re⁡z≤∣z∣\operatorname{Re}z\le\abs zRez≤∣z∣ and Cauchy–Schwarz: ∥u+v∥2=∥u∥2+2Re⁡⟨u,v⟩+∥v∥2≤∥u∥2+2∣⟨u,v⟩∣+∥v∥2≤(∥u∥+∥v∥)2.\norm{u+v}^2=\norm u^2+2\operatorname{Re}\inner{u}{v}+\norm v^2 \le\norm u^2+2\abs{\inner{u}{v}}+\norm v^2 \le\bigl(\norm u+\norm v\bigr)^2 .∥u+v∥2=∥u∥2+2Re⟨u,v⟩+∥v∥2≤∥u∥2+2∣⟨u,v⟩∣+∥v∥2≤(∥u∥+∥v∥)2. Taking square roots gives the triangle inequality. 

Expanding ∥u±v∥2\norm{u\pm v}^2∥u±v∥2 gives two more useful identities: the parallelogram law and the polarization identity.

Proposition 7.31 (Parallelogram law and polarization identity). In a complex inner product space, for all u,vu,vu,v, the parallelogram law ∥u+v∥2+∥u−v∥2=2∥u∥2+2∥v∥2\norm{u+v}^2+\norm{u-v}^2=2\norm u^2+2\norm v^2∥u+v∥2+∥u−v∥2=2∥u∥2+2∥v∥2 holds. Moreover, the inner product can be recovered from the norm by the polarization identity ⟨u,v⟩=14∑α∈{1,−1,i,−i}α ∥v+αu∥2.\inner{u}{v}=\tfrac14\sum_{\alpha\in\set{1,-1,i,-i}} \alpha\,\norm{v+\alpha u}^2 .⟨u,v⟩=41​α∈{1,−1,i,−i}∑​α∥v+αu∥2.

Proof.

  1. Parallelogram law. Expanding ∥u±v∥2=⟨u±v,u±v⟩\norm{u\pm v}^2=\inner{u\pm v}{u\pm v}∥u±v∥2=⟨u±v,u±v⟩ gives ∥u±v∥2=∥u∥2+∥v∥2±2ℜ⟨u,v⟩.\norm{u\pm v}^2=\norm u^2+\norm v^2\pm2\Re\inner u v .∥u±v∥2=∥u∥2+∥v∥2±2ℜ⟨u,v⟩. Adding the two equations (with the plus and with the minus sign) cancels the cross terms and gives the parallelogram law.
  2. Polarization identity. Each α∈{1,−1,i,−i}\alpha\in\set{1,-1,i,-i}α∈{1,−1,i,−i} has ∣α∣=1\abs\alpha=1∣α∣=1, so the same expansion gives ∥v+αu∥2=∥v∥2+∥u∥2+α⟨v,u⟩+α‾ ⟨u,v⟩.\norm{v+\alpha u}^2=\norm v^2+\norm u^2+\alpha\inner v u+\conj\alpha\,\inner u v .∥v+αu∥2=∥v∥2+∥u∥2+α⟨v,u⟩+α⟨u,v⟩. Multiplying by α\alphaα and summing over all four values of α\alphaα, we use ∑αα=0\sum_\alpha\alpha=0∑α​α=0, ∑αα2=0\sum_\alpha\alpha^2=0∑α​α2=0 and ∑ααα‾=4\sum_\alpha\alpha\conj\alpha=4∑α​αα=4. What remains is ∑αα ∥v+αu∥2=4⟨u,v⟩\sum_\alpha\alpha\,\norm{v+\alpha u}^2=4\inner u v∑α​α∥v+αu∥2=4⟨u,v⟩.

 

The polarization identity shows that the inner product is completely determined by the lengths of vectors. In a real space it simplifies to ⟨u,v⟩=14(∥u+v∥2−∥u−v∥2)\inner{u}{v}=\tfrac14\bigl(\norm{u+v}^2-\norm{u-v}^2\bigr)⟨u,v⟩=41​(∥u+v∥2−∥u−v∥2). In a real space we can also define the angle θ\thetaθ between nonzero vectors: cos⁡θ=⟨u,v⟩∥u∥ ∥v∥.\cos\theta=\frac{\inner{u}{v}}{\norm u\,\norm v}.cosθ=∥u∥∥v∥⟨u,v⟩​. By Cauchy–Schwarz the right-hand side lies in [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1], so such an angle exists.

The parallelogram law helps to recognize whether a given measure of length can come from some inner product. If the law fails, it cannot (Example 7.35).

Remark 7.36 (The Jordan–von Neumann theorem [self-study]). The converse is also true. States, amplitudes, and what comes next

Let us sum up what the ideas of this lecture mean in quantum mechanics. A physical state is described by a unit vector ∣ψ⟩\ket{\psi}∣ψ⟩. The vectors ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ and eiθ∣ψ⟩e^{i\theta}\ket\psieiθ∣ψ⟩ describe the same state, because the common factor eiθe^{i\theta}eiθ, called a phase, changes no probabilities. The number ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is the probability of finding the system in the state ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩. A measurement never confuses orthogonal states (⟨ϕ | ψ⟩=0\braket{\phi}{\psi}=0⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩=0): a system in the state ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ is never found in the state ∣ϕ⟩\ket\phi∣ϕ⟩. In Lecture 9 we prove that eigenvectors of an observable with different eigenvalues are orthogonal. So different measurement outcomes correspond to orthogonal states.

For example, ⟨+ | −⟩=12(⟨0 | 0⟩−⟨1 | 1⟩)=0\braket{+}{-}=\tfrac12(\braket00-\braket11)=0⟨+∣−⟩=21​(⟨0∣0⟩−⟨1∣1⟩)=0, so a system in the state ∣+⟩\ket+∣+⟩ is never found in the state ∣−⟩\ket-∣−⟩. But the inner product of each of them with ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ equals 12\tfrac1{\sqrt2}2​1​, as in Example 7.11. So a measurement in the basis ∣0⟩,∣1⟩\ket0,\ket1∣0⟩,∣1⟩ gives each outcome with probability 12\tfrac1221​ in both cases. Such a measurement therefore does not tell ∣+⟩\ket+∣+⟩ and ∣−⟩\ket-∣−⟩ apart, although they differ only in the sign in front of ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩. A measurement in the basis ∣+⟩,∣−⟩\ket+,\ket-∣+⟩,∣−⟩ does tell them apart.

Remark 7.38 (Toward Hilbert space [self-study]). In quantum mechanics the state space is called a Hilbert space. It is a complex inner product space with one more property, completeness: every sequence of vectors whose terms get arbitrarily close to each other has a limit in the same space. Every finite-dimensional inner product space is complete, so the difference is invisible in our lectures. For infinite-dimensional spaces, such as function spaces, completeness is an extra requirement. It is studied in Lecture 13.

Example 7.39 (The closest point on a line). Let eee be a unit vector and uuu any vector. Which multiple cecece of eee is closest to uuu? The error u−⟨e,u⟩ eu-\inner e u\,eu−⟨e,u⟩e is orthogonal to eee, as in the proof of Cauchy–Schwarz. The vector u−ceu-ceu−ce is the sum of this error and a multiple of eee, so by the Pythagorean theorem ∥u−ce∥2=∥u−⟨e,u⟩ e∥2+∣c−⟨e,u⟩∣2.\norm{u-ce}^2=\norm{u-\inner eu\,e}^2+\abs{c-\inner eu}^2 .∥u−ce∥2=∥u−⟨e,u⟩e∥2+∣c−⟨e,u⟩∣2. This expression is smallest when c=⟨e,u⟩c=\inner e uc=⟨e,u⟩. So the closest vector is ⟨e,u⟩ e\inner{e}{u}\,e⟨e,u⟩e, the orthogonal projection of uuu onto the line in the direction of eee. The least-squares method and the orthonormal expansions of Lecture 8 rest on this idea.

In the next lecture we answer the question: does every inner product space have a basis of pairwise orthogonal unit vectors, that is, an orthonormal basis? In finite dimensions the answer is yes: the Gram–Schmidt process constructs one. In an orthonormal basis the inner product becomes the familiar sum ∑kuk‾vk\sum_k\conj{u_k}v_k∑k​uk​​vk​, and the matrices of operators become especially simple.

An inner product is conjugate symmetric, positive definite, and linear in the second slot. Hence it is conjugate-linear in the first slot, that is, sesquilinear (the physics convention). In Dirac notation ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket and conjugate-linear in the bra. The inner product defines the norm ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm v=\sqrt{\inner v v}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​ and orthogonality ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner u v=0⟨u,v⟩=0, and the Pythagorean theorem links them. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥\abs{\inner u v}\le\norm u\norm v∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥ follows from the orthogonal decomposition and the Pythagorean theorem. The triangle inequality follows from it. The parallelogram law and the polarization identity show that the inner product is determined by the lengths of vectors. In quantum mechanics states are unit vectors, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket\phi\psi}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is a probability, and a measurement always tells orthogonal states apart. These ideas are needed for the spectral theorem in Lecture 10.

  • Inner product, physics convention — conjugate symmetric, linear in the second slot, positive definite; ⟨ϕ | ψ⟩\braket{\phi}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩ is linear in the ket.
  • Norm and orthogonality — ∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩\norm v=\sqrt{\inner v v}∥v∥=⟨v,v⟩​; ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner u v=0⟨u,v⟩=0; the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Cauchy–Schwarz inequality — ∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥ ∥v∥\abs{\inner u v}\le\norm u\,\norm v∣⟨u,v⟩∣≤∥u∥∥v∥; the modulus of an amplitude is at most one.
  • Triangle inequality, parallelogram law, and polarization — properties of lengths and distances; ⟨⋅,⋅⟩\inner{\cdot}{\cdot}⟨⋅,⋅⟩ is recovered from ∥⋅∥\norm{\cdot}∥⋅∥.
  • Physical picture — states are unit vectors, ∣⟨ϕ | ψ⟩∣2\abs{\braket{\phi}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ϕ∣ψ⟩∣2 is a probability, orthogonality means distinguishability.
