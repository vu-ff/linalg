In this lecture we meet the Gram–Schmidt process and show that every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. We will apply orthonormal bases to two problems. First, orthogonal projection will find the point of a subspace closest to a given vector (the best approximation). Second, the Riesz representation theorem will show that every linear functional is a bra. These are the daily tools of quantum mechanics: a state is expanded in an eigenbasis, and the probabilities of measurement outcomes are squared moduli of inner products.

Orthonormal bases

Definition 8.1 (Orthonormal list and basis). A list e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​ is orthonormal if ⟨ej,ek⟩=δjk,\inner{e_j}{e_k}=\delta_{jk},⟨ej​,ek​⟩=δjk​, i.e. each vector is a unit vector and distinct vectors are orthogonal. An orthonormal list that is also a basis is an orthonormal basis.

In an orthonormal basis, finding coordinates requires no linear system: the coordinates are inner products.

Corollary 8.14 (Extending to an orthonormal basis). Every orthonormal list in a finite-dimensional VVV extends to an orthonormal basis of VVV.

Proof. The orthonormal list is linearly independent (Lemma 8.2), so it extends to a basis (Lecture 2). Run Gram–Schmidt on that basis. The already-orthonormal vectors are unchanged, and the rest become an orthonormal completion. 

By Gram–Schmidt, every nnn-dimensional complex inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and by Corollary 8.4 the coordinates in it turn the inner product into the standard one. So Cn\C^nCn with its standard inner product is the model of every such space: each is isometric to it, i.e. isomorphic by a map that preserves not only the linear structure but also inner products, and hence norms.

Measurement, expansion, and what comes next

In quantum mechanics the possible outcomes of a measurement usually correspond to the vectors of an orthonormal basis. Lectures 9–10 show that the eigenvectors of an observable (the operator describing a measured quantity) can form exactly such a basis.

Suppose that the eigenvalues λ1,…,λn\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_nλ1​,…,λn​ of an observable are distinct, and that the corresponding eigenvectors ∣e1⟩,…,∣en⟩\ket{e_1},\dots,\ket{e_n}∣e1​⟩,…,∣en​⟩ form an orthonormal basis. An ideal (projective) measurement can return only one of the values λk\lambda_kλk​. Expand a normalized state ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩: ∣ψ⟩=∑k⟨ek,ψ⟩∣ek⟩.\ket\psi=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\psi}\ket{e_k} .∣ψ⟩=k∑​⟨ek​,ψ⟩∣ek​⟩. 6 In ℂ², e₁=½(1+i,1−i) is a unit vector. The Gram–Schmidt process continues with v₂=(0,1). What is w₂=v₂−⟨e₁,v₂⟩e₁?

7 The Gram–Schmidt process is run on v₁, v₂, v₃, where v₁, v₂ are linearly independent and v₃=v₁−2v₂. What happens at the third step?

10 Let p be the orthogonal projection of v onto a subspace U, with ‖v−p‖=5. A vector u∈U has ‖p−u‖=12. What is ‖v−u‖?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. v−p∈U⊥ and p−u∈U, so by the Pythagorean theorem ‖v−u‖²=5²+12²=169, and ‖v−u‖=13. This is the proof of the best-approximation theorem: every other u∈U is farther from v than p. Adding the distances, 5+12=17, would treat the two pieces as parallel; they are perpendicular.

11 e₁, e₂ are orthonormal in ℂ³, ‖v‖=3 and ⟨e₁,v⟩=2. Which number can ⟨e₂,v⟩ be?

12 The system Ax=b with A=(1,1,1)ᵀ and b=(1,2,6) asks for x=1, x=2 and x=6 at once. Which x minimizes ‖Ax−b‖?

14 |ψ⟩=(3|0⟩+4i|1⟩)/5 is measured in the orthonormal basis |0⟩,|1⟩, and |1⟩ is found. With what probability, and what is the state right after the measurement?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Born rule: the probability is |⟨1|ψ⟩|².
