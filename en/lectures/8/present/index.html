Lecture 8

Orthonormal Bases, Gram–Schmidt, and Riesz

By the end of this lecture you will be able to

  • Expand vectors in an orthonormal basis, with coordinates as inner products.
  • Build an orthonormal basis by the Gram–Schmidt process.
  • Compute orthogonal projections.
  • Solve best-approximation and least-squares problems.
  • Link the Riesz representation theorem to the bra–ket correspondence.

In this lecture we meet the Gram–Schmidt process and show that every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. We will apply orthonormal bases to two problems. First, orthogonal projection will find the point of a subspace closest to a given vector (the best approximation). Second, the Riesz representation theorem will show that every linear functional is a bra. These are the daily tools of quantum mechanics: a state is expanded in an eigenbasis, and the probabilities of measurement outcomes are squared moduli of inner products.

Orthonormal bases

Definition 8.1 (Orthonormal list and basis). A list e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​ is orthonormal if ⟨ej,ek⟩=δjk,\inner{e_j}{e_k}=\delta_{jk},⟨ej​,ek​⟩=δjk​, i.e. each vector is a unit vector and distinct vectors are orthogonal. An orthonormal list that is also a basis is an orthonormal basis.

Lemma 8.2 (Orthonormal lists are independent). Every orthonormal list is linearly independent.

Proof. Let ∑kckek=0\sum_k c_ke_k=0∑k​ck​ek​=0. Pairing this equation with eje_jej​ shows that every coefficient vanishes: 0=⟨ej,∑kckek⟩=∑kckδjk=cj.0=\inner{e_j}{\sum_k c_ke_k}=\sum_k c_k\delta_{jk}=c_j. 0=⟨ej​,k∑​ck​ek​⟩=k∑​ck​δjk​=cj​. 

In an orthonormal basis, finding coordinates requires no linear system: the coordinates are inner products.

Proof. The eke_kek​ form a basis, so v=∑kckekv=\sum_k c_ke_kv=∑k​ck​ek​. Pairing with eje_jej​ and using orthonormality gives ⟨ej,v⟩=∑kckδjk=cj\inner{e_j}{v}=\sum_k c_k\delta_{jk}=c_j⟨ej​,v⟩=∑k​ck​δjk​=cj​, which is the first formula. Corollary 8.4 (Inner products from coordinates). In an orthonormal basis ⟨u,v⟩=∑k⟨ek,u⟩‾ ⟨ek,v⟩.\inner{u}{v}=\sum_k\conj{\inner{e_k}{u}}\,\inner{e_k}{v}.⟨u,v⟩=k∑​⟨ek​,u⟩​⟨ek​,v⟩.

Proof. Expand u=∑j⟨ej,u⟩eju=\sum_j\inner{e_j}{u}e_ju=∑j​⟨ej​,u⟩ej​ and v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​. So in an orthonormal basis the inner product is always the standard sum of coordinates ∑kak‾bk.\sum_k\conj{a_k}b_k .k∑​ak​​bk​.

Remark 8.5 (Completeness relation). In Dirac notation the expansion reads ∣v⟩=∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek,v⟩=(∑k∣ek⟩ ⁣⟨ek∣)∣v⟩,\ket v=\sum_k\ket{e_k}\inner{e_k}{v} =\Bigl(\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}\Bigr)\ket v,∣v⟩=k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​,v⟩=(k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣)∣v⟩, so an orthonormal basis satisfies the completeness relation ∑k∣ek⟩ ⁣⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. In quantum mechanics this resolution of the identity is routinely inserted between two symbols: that is how any expression is written in coordinates of a chosen basis.

The Gram–Schmidt process

Theorem 8.9 (Gram–Schmidt). Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. Concretely, from any basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ the formulas e1=v1∥v1∥,ek=wk∥wk∥,wk=vk−∑j<k⟨ej,vk⟩ ej,e_1=\frac{v_1}{\norm{v_1}},\qquad\allowbreak e_k=\frac{w_k}{\norm{w_k}},\qquad\allowbreak w_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\inner{e_j}{v_k}\,e_j ,e1​=∥v1​∥v1​​,ek​=∥wk​∥wk​​,wk​=vk​−j<k∑​⟨ej​,vk​⟩ej​, build an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with span⁡(e1,…,ek)=span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(e_1,\dots,e_k)=\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(e1​,…,ek​)=span(v1​,…,vk​) for every kkk.

Proof. We prove the theorem by induction on kkk.

  1. For k=1k=1k=1, the vector v1v_1v1​ is nonzero, because it belongs to a basis. Hence e1=v1/∥v1∥e_1=v_1/\norm{v_1}e1​=v1​/∥v1​∥ is a unit vector and span⁡(e1)=span⁡(v1)\spn(e_1)=\spn(v_1)span(e1​)=span(v1​).

  2. Assume that e1,…,ek−1e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}e1​,…,ek−1​ are already orthonormal and span the same subspace as the vectors v1,…,vk−1v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}v1​,…,vk−1​.

  3. The vector wkw_kwk​ is nonzero. If wk=0w_k=0wk​=0, the vector vkv_kvk​ would be a linear combination of e1,…,ek−1e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}e1​,…,ek−1​, and hence also of v1,…,vk−1v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}v1​,…,vk−1​. This contradicts the linear independence of v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​. Hence ek=wk/∥wk∥e_k=w_k/\norm{w_k}ek​=wk​/∥wk​∥ is defined and is a unit vector.

  4. Orthogonality. For i<ki<ki<k, ⟨ei,wk⟩=⟨ei,vk⟩−∑j<k⟨ej,vk⟩⟨ei,ej⟩=⟨ei,vk⟩−⟨ei,vk⟩=0,\inner{e_i}{w_k}=\inner{e_i}{v_k}-\sum_{j<k}\inner{e_j}{v_k}\inner{e_i}{e_j} =\inner{e_i}{v_k}-\inner{e_i}{v_k}=0,⟨ei​,wk​⟩=⟨ei​,vk​⟩−j<k∑​⟨ej​,vk​⟩⟨ei​,ej​⟩=⟨ei​,vk​⟩−⟨ei​,vk​⟩=0, so eke_kek​ is orthogonal to all the earlier vectors.

Corollary 8.14 (Extending to an orthonormal basis). Every orthonormal list in a finite-dimensional VVV extends to an orthonormal basis of VVV.

Proof. The orthonormal list is linearly independent (Lemma 8.2), so it extends to a basis (Lecture 2). Run Gram–Schmidt on that basis. The already-orthonormal vectors are unchanged, and the rest become an orthonormal completion. 

By Gram–Schmidt, every nnn-dimensional complex inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and by Corollary 8.4 the coordinates in it turn the inner product into the standard one. So Cn\C^nCn with its standard inner product is the model of every such space: each is isometric to it, i.e. isomorphic by a map that preserves not only the linear structure but also inner products, and hence norms.

Orthogonal projection and best approximation

Definition 8.15 (Orthogonal projection). Let UUU be a subspace of VVV with orthonormal basis e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​. The orthogonal projection onto UUU is the operator PUv=∑k=1m⟨ek,v⟩ ek.P_U v=\sum_{k=1}^m\inner{e_k}{v}\,e_k .PU​v=k=1∑m​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​.

Theorem 8.16 (Orthogonal decomposition). For each v∈Vv\in Vv∈V, v−PUv∈U⊥v-P_Uv\in U^\perpv−PU​v∈U⊥, and V=U⊕U⊥V=U\oplus U^\perpV=U⊕U⊥. Thus PUP_UPU​ is the projection onto UUU along U⊥U^\perpU⊥.

Proof. For each iii, ⟨ei,v−PUv⟩=⟨ei,v⟩−∑k⟨ek,v⟩⟨ei,ek⟩=⟨ei,v⟩−⟨ei,v⟩=0.\inner{e_i}{v-P_Uv}=\inner{e_i}{v}-\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}\inner{e_i}{e_k} =\inner{e_i}{v}-\inner{e_i}{v}=0 .⟨ei​,v−PU​v⟩=⟨ei​,v⟩−k∑​⟨ek​,v⟩⟨ei​,ek​⟩=⟨ei​,v⟩−⟨ei​,v⟩=0. So v−PUvv-P_Uvv−PU​v is orthogonal to every eie_iei​, and hence to UUU. The identity v=PUv+(v−PUv)v=P_Uv+(v-P_Uv)v=PU​v+(v−PU​v) writes vvv as the sum of a vector of UUU and a vector of U⊥U^\perpU⊥. Moreover U∩U⊥={0}U\cap U^\perp=\set0U∩U⊥={0}, since a vector orthogonal to itself is zero. Hence the sum is direct. 

The formula uses a basis, but the operator does not depend on it. By Theorem 8.16, every vector vvv is uniquely the sum of a vector of UUU and a vector of U⊥U^\perpU⊥, and PUvP_UvPU​v is exactly the first summand. No basis appears in this description, so any other orthonormal basis of UUU yields the same operator PUP_UPU​. The vector ppp of Figure 1 is PUv3P_Uv_3PU​v3​ for U=span⁡(e1,e2)U=\spn(e_1,e_2)U=span(e1​,e2​): each Gram–Schmidt step subtracts the projection onto the span of the vectors already built.

Proposition 8.17 (Dimension of the orthogonal complement). For a subspace UUU of a finite-dimensional VVV, dim⁡U+dim⁡U⊥=dim⁡V\dim U+\dim U^\perp=\dim VdimU+dimU⊥=dimV, and (U⊥)⊥=U(U^\perp)^\perp=U(U⊥)⊥=U.

Proof. Take an orthonormal basis e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​ of UUU and extend it to an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ of VVV (Corollary 8.14). The extra vectors em+1,…,ene_{m+1},\dots,e_nem+1​,…,en​ lie in U⊥U^\perpU⊥. They also span U⊥U^\perpU⊥, because any v∈U⊥v\in U^\perpv∈U⊥ has zero coordinates along e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​. So dim⁡U⊥=n−m\dim U^\perp=n-mdimU⊥=n−m.

For the second claim, the inclusion U⊆(U⊥)⊥U\subseteq(U^\perp)^\perpU⊆(U⊥)⊥ is immediate from the definition: every u∈Uu\in Uu∈U is orthogonal to every vector of U⊥U^\perpU⊥. Applying the dimension identity to U⊥U^\perpU⊥ gives dim⁡(U⊥)⊥=n−(n−m)=m=dim⁡U\dim(U^\perp)^\perp=n-(n-m)=m=\dim Udim(U⊥)⊥=n−(n−m)=m=dimU. A subspace contained in another of the same finite dimension equals it, so (U⊥)⊥=U(U^\perp)^\perp=U(U⊥)⊥=U.

The Riesz representation theorem

Recall that a linear functional on VVV is a linear map φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F to the scalars, F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C} (Lecture 7). In an inner product space every linear functional is the inner product with a fixed vector.

Theorem 8.25 (Riesz representation). Let VVV be finite-dimensional. For every linear functional φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F there is a unique w∈Vw\in Vw∈V such that φ(v)=⟨w,v⟩for all v∈V.\varphi(v)=\inner{w}{v}\qquad\allowbreak \text{for all }v\in V.φ(v)=⟨w,v⟩for all v∈V.

Proof. Fix an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ and set w=∑kφ(ek)‾ ekw=\sum_k\conj{\varphi(e_k)}\,e_kw=∑k​φ(ek​)​ek​. Then for any v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​, ⟨w,v⟩=∑kφ(ek)⟨ek,v⟩=φ(∑k⟨ek,v⟩ek)=φ(v).\inner{w}{v}=\sum_k\varphi(e_k)\inner{e_k}{v} =\varphi\Bigl(\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_k\Bigr)=\varphi(v).⟨w,v⟩=k∑​φ(ek​)⟨ek​,v⟩=φ(k∑​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​)=φ(v). The first step uses ⟨w,ek⟩=φ(ek)\inner{w}{e_k}=\varphi(e_k)⟨w,ek​⟩=φ(ek​), and the second the linearity of φ\varphiφ. For uniqueness, if ⟨w,v⟩=⟨w′,v⟩\inner{w}{v}=\inner{w'}{v}⟨w,v⟩=⟨w′,v⟩ for all vvv, then ⟨w−w′,v⟩=0\inner{w-w'}{v}=0⟨w−w′,v⟩=0 for all vvv. Taking v=w−w′v=w-w'v=w−w′ gives w=w′w=w'w=w′. 

Remark 8.26 (Bra–ket correspondence). The Riesz theorem says that every linear functional φ\varphiφ is a bra: there is a unique ket ∣w⟩\ket w∣w⟩ with φ=⟨w∣\varphi=\bra wφ=⟨w∣. So the map ∣w⟩↦⟨w∣\ket w\mapsto\bra w∣w⟩↦⟨w∣ is a bijection. As we saw in Lecture 7, it is conjugate-linear: a ∣w⟩+b ∣u⟩↦a‾ ⟨w∣+b‾ ⟨u∣.a\,\ket{w} + b\,\ket{u} \mapsto \conj{a}\,\bra{w} + \conj{b}\,\bra{u}.a∣w⟩+b∣u⟩↦a⟨w∣+b⟨u∣. This is the essence of Dirac’s notation.

Measurement, expansion, and what comes next

In quantum mechanics the possible outcomes of a measurement usually correspond to the vectors of an orthonormal basis. Lectures 9–10 show that the eigenvectors of an observable (the operator describing a measured quantity) can form exactly such a basis.

Suppose that the eigenvalues λ1,…,λn\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_nλ1​,…,λn​ of an observable are distinct, and that the corresponding eigenvectors ∣e1⟩,…,∣en⟩\ket{e_1},\dots,\ket{e_n}∣e1​⟩,…,∣en​⟩ form an orthonormal basis. An ideal (projective) measurement can return only one of the values λk\lambda_kλk​. Expand a normalized state ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩: ∣ψ⟩=∑k⟨ek,ψ⟩∣ek⟩.\ket\psi=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\psi}\ket{e_k} .∣ψ⟩=k∑​⟨ek​,ψ⟩∣ek​⟩. By the Born rule (Lecture 7), the outcome λk\lambda_kλk​ has probability ∣⟨ek,ψ⟩∣2\abs{\inner{e_k}{\psi}}^2∣⟨ek​,ψ⟩∣2, and Parseval’s relation guarantees that the probabilities sum to ∥ψ∥2=1\norm\psi^2=1∥ψ∥2=1. If the outcome λk\lambda_kλk​ is obtained, the state is replaced by its orthogonal projection onto the line spanned by ∣ek⟩\ket{e_k}∣ek​⟩, divided by its norm; that is ∣ek⟩\ket{e_k}∣ek​⟩ up to a phase factor. When an eigenvalue is repeated, the state is projected onto the whole eigenspace (Lecture 10). The completeness relation ∑k∣ek⟩ ⁣⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I lets us insert a basis anywhere in a calculation.

Example 8.30 (A projective measurement). Let a spin be in the state ∣ψ⟩=12∣0⟩+12∣1⟩,\ket\psi=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket0+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket1,∣ψ⟩=2​1​∣0⟩+2​1​∣1⟩, measured in the basis ∣0⟩,∣1⟩\ket0,\ket1∣0⟩,∣1⟩. The measurement finds the system in the state ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ with probability ∣⟨0,ψ⟩∣2=12\abs{\inner0\psi}^2=\tfrac12∣⟨0,ψ⟩∣2=21​ and in the state ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩ with probability 12\tfrac1221​. If ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ is found, the new spin state immediately after the measurement is the normalized projection ∣ψ⟩  ⟼  P0∣ψ⟩/∥P0∣ψ⟩∥=∣0⟩,P0=∣0⟩ ⁣⟨0∣.\ket\psi\;\longmapsto\;P_0\ket\psi/\norm{P_0\ket\psi}=\ket0, \qquad\allowbreak P_0=\ketbra00 .∣ψ⟩⟼P0​∣ψ⟩/∥P0​∣ψ⟩∥=∣0⟩,P0​=∣0⟩⟨0∣. This update of the state is called the Lüders rule. Hence an immediate repeat of the measurement finds ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ again with probability 111.

Example 8.31 (Expansion in an energy eigenbasis). Let the vectors ∣En⟩\ket{E_n}∣En​⟩ form an orthonormal eigenbasis of a time-independent energy operator (the Hamiltonian), with real eigenvalues EnE_nEn​ (the energies). Lectures 9–10 prove that a Hamiltonian has both properties. Then ∣ψ⟩=∑n⟨En,ψ⟩∣En⟩.\ket\psi=\sum_n\inner{E_n}{\psi}\ket{E_n} .∣ψ⟩=n∑​⟨En​,ψ⟩∣En​⟩. In Lecture 6 we saw how the state evolves in time: ∣ψ(t)⟩=∑ne−iEnt/ℏ⟨En,ψ⟩∣En⟩.\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\inner{E_n}{\psi}\ket{E_n} .∣ψ(t)⟩=n∑​e−iEn​t/ℏ⟨En​,ψ⟩∣En​⟩. This is conservation of energy.

Remark 8.32 (Infinite-dimensional spaces). In an infinite-dimensional space an infinite orthonormal list need not be a basis. Parseval’s relation may then fail, and only Bessel’s inequality remains. Moreover, the decomposition V=U⊕U⊥V=U\oplus U^\perpV=U⊕U⊥ need not hold for every subspace UUU. Lecture 13 repairs both with completeness: of the space (Hilbert spaces), of the orthonormal set, and closedness of the subspace.

In the next lecture the Riesz theorem gives every operator TTT an adjoint T†T^{\adj}T†, characterized by ⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩.

An orthonormal basis satisfies ⟨ej,ek⟩=δjk\inner{e_j}{e_k}=\delta_{jk}⟨ej​,ek​⟩=δjk​. In it, coordinates are inner products, v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​, the norm obeys Parseval’s relation ∥v∥2=∑k∣⟨ek,v⟩∣2\norm v^2=\sum_k\abs{\inner{e_k}{v}}^2∥v∥2=∑k​∣⟨ek​,v⟩∣2, and ∑k∣ek⟩ ⁣⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. Gram–Schmidt builds such a basis from any basis and preserves the partial spans. So every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis, and every orthonormal list extends to one. Orthogonal projection PUv=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ekP_Uv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_kPU​v=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​ gives the orthogonal decomposition V=U⊕U⊥V=U\oplus U^\perpV=U⊕U⊥ and solves the best-approximation problem; Bessel’s inequality is a corollary. Riesz writes every linear functional as ⟨w,⋅⟩\inner{w}{\cdot}⟨w,⋅⟩. This is the bra–ket correspondence.
