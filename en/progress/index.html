Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. tr A=7 and det A=10−4=6, so χ_A(λ)=λ²−7λ+6=(λ−6)(λ−1). The eigenvalues are 6 and 1. Check: 6+1=7=tr A and 6·1=6=det A.

Lecture 5 · Computing eigenvalues and eigenspaces

0 A 3×3 matrix A has the eigenvalues 1 and 4, and 4 is a double root of χ_A. What is tr A?

The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, each listed as many times as its multiplicity.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. tr A=λ₁+λ₂+λ₃=1+4+4=9. Counting the double root only once gives 5, which is wrong.

Lecture 5 · Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent

0 Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are…

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The proof uses only Tv=λv and the fact that the eigenvalues differ. Distinct eigenvalues force independence over ℝ and ℂ alike, and a zero eigenvalue is allowed. They need not span V: the eigenvectors (1,0,0) and (0,1,0) of diag(1,2,3) span only a plane in ℝ³.

Lecture 5 · Existence of eigenvalues over C\CC

0 How many real eigenvalues does a rotation of ℝ² by 90° have?

An eigenvector is a direction the map does not turn.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A quarter turn moves every non-zero vector off its line, so there is no real eigenvector. Over ℂ the same matrix has the eigenvalues ±i.

Lecture 5 · Existence of eigenvalues over C\CC

0 V≠{0} is a finite-dimensional complex vector space and T∈ℒ(V). What is guaranteed?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. What does the fundamental theorem of algebra say about χ_T? χ_T has degree dim V≥1, so it has a complex root, and that root is an eigenvalue. A basis of eigenvectors is not guaranteed: the shear [[1,1],[0,1]] has only one eigendirection.

Lecture 5 · Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

0 A=[[2,1,0],[0,2,0],[0,0,2]]. Its only eigenvalue 2 has algebraic multiplicity 3. What is its geometric multiplicity?

The geometric multiplicity is dim E(2,A)=dim ker(A−2I). Reduce A−2I and count the free variables.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A−2I=[[0,1,0],[0,0,0],[0,0,0]] is already in RREF, with its only pivot in column 2. So x₂=0, the free variables are x₁ and x₃, and E(2,A)=span((1,0,0),(0,0,1)) has dimension 2<3. So A is defective.

Lecture 5 · Upper triangular matrices

0 An upper-triangular 4×4 matrix has diagonal entries 1, 1, 3, 5. How many distinct eigenvalues does it have?

The diagonal of a triangular matrix is its list of eigenvalues, with repeats.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The eigenvalues are 1, 1, 3, 5, so the distinct ones are 1, 3 and 5 — three of them.

Lecture 5 · Upper triangular matrices

0 An upper-triangular 3×3 matrix A has diagonal entries 2, 0, 5. Which statement is true?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. det A=2·0·5=0, so A is not invertible. The diagonal of a triangular matrix lists its eigenvalues, so 0 is one of them. The entries above the diagonal affect neither.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 An operator T∈ℒ(V) is diagonalizable exactly when…

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. What does a diagonal matrix say about each basis vector? That is the definition: in a basis of eigenvectors the matrix is diagonal, and conversely. dim V distinct eigenvalues suffice but are not needed (the identity has one eigenvalue). On a non-zero finite-dimensional complex space every operator has an eigenvalue and a triangular matrix, yet the shear is not diagonalizable.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 An n×n matrix over 𝔽 has n distinct eigenvalues in 𝔽. It is…

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Count linearly independent eigenvectors. Distinct eigenvalues give n linearly independent eigenvectors, which is exactly a basis of eigenvectors. A zero eigenvalue changes nothing. The converse fails: a repeated eigenvalue may still diagonalize.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 A 3×3 matrix A has the eigenvalues 3, 2, 2, listed with multiplicity. Which condition is equivalent to A being diagonalizable?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Diagonalizable means the eigenspace dimensions add up to 3. What is dim E(3,A)? The eigenvalue 3 has algebraic multiplicity 1, and geometric ≤ algebraic, so dim E(3,A)=1. Hence the dimensions add up to 3 exactly when dim E(2,A)=2. A repeated eigenvalue alone decides nothing: the lecture's [[3,1,0],[0,2,0],[0,0,2]] has dim E(2,A)=2 and diagonalizes, while [[3,0,0],[0,2,1],[0,0,2]] has dim E(2,A)=1 and does not. det A=3·2·2=12≠0 holds for both, so invertibility is not the condition.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 A=[[1,1],[0,1]] has the single eigenvalue 1. Which fact shows that A is not diagonalizable?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Solve (A−I)v=0 and find the dimension of its solution set. The eigenvectors lie only along (1,0), so dim E(1,A)=1<2: one eigendirection cannot make a basis of ℝ², and no P diagonalizes A. det A=1 and tr A=2 are true, but the diagonalizable identity matrix I has the same determinant and trace.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 Which matrix P gives P⁻¹AP=diag(3,−1) for A=[[1,2],[2,1]]?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The columns of P are eigenvectors, in the same order as the eigenvalues on the diagonal of D. AP=PD says, column by column, that Avₖ=λₖvₖ. So the first column must be an eigenvector for 3 and the second one for −1: A(1,1)=(3,3)=3(1,1) and A(1,−1)=(−1,1)=−(1,−1), so P=[[1,1],[1,−1]]. Swapping the columns gives diag(−1,3). Neither A itself nor D=diag(3,−1) is built from eigenvectors.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 A=PDP⁻¹ with D=diag(2,−1). What is tr A⁴?

A⁴=PD⁴P⁻¹, and similar matrices have the same trace.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The middle factors cancel, so A⁴=PD⁴P⁻¹ with D⁴=diag(16,1). The trace does not change under similarity (Lecture 4), so tr A⁴=16+1=17, whatever P is.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 V≠{0} is a finite-dimensional complex vector space, and S,T∈ℒ(V) commute. What is guaranteed?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. An eigenspace E(λ,S) is invariant under T. T maps E(λ,S) into itself, so T has an eigenvector inside it, and that vector is an eigenvector of S too. A common basis also needs both operators to be diagonalizable: the identity commutes with the shear, which has no basis of eigenvectors. I and 2I commute but share no eigenvalue.

Lecture 6 · The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

0 A is 2×2 with tr A=3 and det A=2. Cayley–Hamilton gives A²=3A−cI. What is c?

For a 2×2 matrix the characteristic polynomial is λ²−(tr A)λ+det A.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. χ_A(λ)=λ²−3λ+2, so A²−3A+2I=0. Hence A²=3A−2I and c=2.

Lecture 6 · The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

0 A matrix A satisfies A²−5A+6I=0. Which matrix is A⁻¹?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Move the constant term to the other side and factor out A. A²−5A=−6I, i.e. A(A−5I)=−6I. Dividing by −6 gives A·(5I−A)/6=I, so A⁻¹=(5I−A)/6. The option (A²−5A)/6 is just −I.

Lecture 6 · Functions of operators

0 T=diag(1,−1), so T²=I. Which statement is true?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Spectral mapping takes eigenvectors of T to eigenvectors of f(T). Does it work backwards when f(1)=f(−1)? f(λ)=λ² sends both 1 and −1 to 1, so T²=I, and every non-zero vector is an eigenvector of I. But T(1,1)=(1,−1), so (1,1) is not an eigenvector of T: its eigendirections are only the two coordinate axes.

Lecture 6 · Diagonalizable operators

0 ẋ=Ax and Av=−2v. What is the solution with x(0)=v?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The solution is x(t)=eᵗᴬv, and a function of A acts on an eigenvector through its eigenvalue. eᵗᴬv=e⁻²ᵗv, so the state stays on the eigendirection and decays exponentially.

Lecture 6 · When diagonalization fails [self-study]

0 Self-study: in the Jordan form of a diagonalizable operator, every block has size…

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A block of size 2 or more contains a generalized eigenvector that is not an eigenvector. Diagonalizable means the Jordan form is already diagonal, which is exactly the statement that every block is 1×1.

Lecture 7 · Inner products

0 In the physics convention, ⟨(2−i)u,iv⟩ equals…

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A scalar leaves the first slot conjugated and the second slot unchanged. The first slot is conjugate-linear, so 2−i comes out as 2+i; the second slot is linear, so i comes out as i. Hence ⟨(2−i)u,iv⟩=(2+i)i⟨u,v⟩=(−1+2i)⟨u,v⟩. Conjugating the second scalar instead (the convention of many mathematics texts) gives −1−2i; conjugating neither gives 1+2i, and conjugating both gives 1−2i.

Lecture 7 · Inner products

0 In ℂ² with the standard inner product, u=(1−i,2) and v=(3,i). What is ⟨u,v⟩?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. ⟨u,v⟩=Σūₖvₖ: only the coordinates of the first vector are conjugated. ⟨u,v⟩=(1+i)·3+2·i=3+3i+2i=3+5i. Conjugating nothing gives 3−i. Conjugating v instead gives 3−5i, which is ⟨v,u⟩, the conjugate of the answer. Conjugating both vectors gives 3+i.

Lecture 7 · Inner products

0 For which weights (w₁,w₂) is ⟨u,v⟩=w₁ū₁v₁+w₂ū₂v₂ an inner product on ℂ²?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Test positive definiteness on the vectors (1,0) and (0,1). Both weights must be positive: then ⟨v,v⟩=w₁|v₁|²+w₂|v₂|² is positive for every v≠0. With (1,0) the non-zero vector (0,1) has ⟨v,v⟩=0, and with (3,−1) it has ⟨v,v⟩=−1, so positive definiteness fails. With (i,1) the number ⟨(1,0),(1,0)⟩=i is not even real, so conjugate symmetry fails.

Lecture 7 · Norm, orthogonality, and the Pythagorean theorem

0 In ℂ³ with the standard inner product, v=(1,i,1+i). What is ‖(3−4i)v‖?

‖av‖=|a|‖v‖, and ‖v‖²=Σ|vₖ|².

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. ‖v‖²=|1|²+|i|²+|1+i|²=1+1+2=4, so ‖v‖=2. Since |3−4i|=√(9+16)=5, ‖(3−4i)v‖=5·2=10. Squaring the coordinates without the modulus gives 1−1+2i=2i, which is not even real.

Lecture 7 · Norm, orthogonality, and the Pythagorean theorem

0 e₁, e₂, e₃ are orthonormal. What is ‖2e₁−3ie₂+6e₃‖?

The three terms are pairwise orthogonal, so their squared norms add up.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. By the Pythagorean theorem for pairwise orthogonal vectors, ‖2e₁−3ie₂+6e₃‖²=|2|²+|−3i|²+|6|²=4+9+36=49, so the norm is 7. Adding the moduli of the coefficients (2+3+6=11) does not apply the theorem: only their squares add up.

Lecture 7 · Inner products

0 Vectors v₁, v₂ have the Gram matrix G=[[2,1+2i],[1−2i,c]]. For which c are v₁ and v₂ linearly dependent?

The vectors are linearly dependent exactly when G is not invertible, that is, when det G=0.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. det G=2c−(1+2i)(1−2i)=2c−5, which is 0 for c=5/2. The product subtracted is |1+2i|²=5, not (1+2i)²: the entry below the diagonal is ⟨v₂,v₁⟩, the conjugate of the entry above it. For a larger c the vectors are independent; a smaller c is impossible, as the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality will show.

Lecture 7 · Norm, orthogonality, and the Pythagorean theorem

0 In ℂ³ with the standard inner product, U=span((1,i,0),(0,1,1)). Which vector lies in U⊥?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. A vector lies in U⊥ when it is orthogonal to both vectors that span U. Conjugate the coordinates in the first slot. ⟨(1,i,0),(i,1,−1)⟩=i+(−i)·1=0 and ⟨(0,1,1),(i,1,−1)⟩=1−1=0. So (i,1,−1) is orthogonal to both spanning vectors, and by conjugate-linearity in the first slot to all of U. (−i,1,−1) passes the first test only if i is not conjugated. (i,1,0) is orthogonal to (1,i,0) but not to (0,1,1), and (0,1,−1) is orthogonal to (0,1,1) but not to (1,i,0).

Lecture 7 · The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

0 In ℂ² with the standard inner product, u=(1,i). For which v does |⟨u,v⟩|=‖u‖‖v‖ hold?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds exactly when u and v are linearly dependent, and in ℂ² the multiple may be complex. (i,−1)=i(1,i) is a complex multiple of u, so equality holds: |⟨u,v⟩|=|2i|=2=√2·√2. The vector (1,−i), with conjugated coordinates, is not a multiple of u; it is even orthogonal to u, and so is (i,1). For (2,i), |⟨u,v⟩|=3<√10=‖u‖‖v‖. The inequality is strict, because the vectors are linearly independent.

Lecture 7 · Triangle inequality, parallelogram law, and polarization

0 Can an inner product on ℝ² induce the length ‖(x,y)‖=|x|+|y|? Test the parallelogram law on u=(2,0) and v=(0,1): what are ‖u+v‖²+‖u−v‖² and 2‖u‖²+2‖v‖²?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Compare ‖u+v‖²+‖u−v‖² with 2‖u‖²+2‖v‖². Square the lengths. ‖u+v‖=‖(2,1)‖=3 and ‖u−v‖=‖(2,−1)‖=3, so the left side is 9+9=18, while 2‖u‖²+2‖v‖²=8+2=10. Every length induced by an inner product satisfies the parallelogram law, so no inner product induces this one. Without the squares both sides are 6, which proves nothing. 10 and 10 are the dot product's values, which measures ‖(2,1)‖=√5, not 3; 9 and 5 drop the term ‖u−v‖² and the factor 2.

Lecture 7 · Triangle inequality, parallelogram law, and polarization

0 In a real inner product space, ‖u+v‖=5 and ‖u−v‖=3. What is ⟨u,v⟩?

The real polarization identity: ⟨u,v⟩=(‖u+v‖²−‖u−v‖²)/4.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. ⟨u,v⟩=(25−9)/4=4. The identity uses squared lengths, so (5−3)/4=1/2 is wrong. Dividing by 2 instead of 4 gives 8: each square contains ±2⟨u,v⟩, so their difference is 4⟨u,v⟩.

Lecture 7 · The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

0 |φ⟩ and |ψ⟩ are unit vectors. Which number can be the amplitude ⟨φ|ψ⟩?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. By Cauchy–Schwarz, |⟨φ|ψ⟩|≤‖φ‖‖ψ‖=1. The bound is on the modulus. |0.5+0.8i|=√0.89≈0.94≤1. The other numbers violate the bound: |0.8+0.7i|=√1.13≈1.06, |1−0.5i|=√1.25≈1.12 and |−1.2i|=1.2. Checking the real and imaginary parts separately is not enough: both parts of 0.8+0.7i are below 1, yet its modulus is above 1.

Lecture 7 · Dirac notation, amplitudes, and what comes next

0 |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthonormal, and |+⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. Which unit vector describes the same physical state as |+⟩?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Only a common factor eⁱᶿ on the whole vector leaves the state unchanged. −(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 is (−1)|+⟩, and −1=eⁱᶿ with θ=π is a phase, so no probability changes. The other three vectors, like |+⟩, give each outcome of a measurement in the basis |0⟩,|1⟩ with probability 1/2, yet they describe other states: each changes the coefficient of only one of |0⟩, |1⟩. A measurement in the basis |+⟩,|−⟩ tells them apart: |⟨+|ψ⟩|² is 1 for −|+⟩, 0 for |−⟩ and 1/2 for the last two.

Lecture 7 · Dirac notation, amplitudes, and what comes next

0 |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthonormal, and |χ⟩=3|0⟩+2i|1⟩. To which vector does the operator |0⟩⟨1| send |χ⟩?

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. |ψ⟩⟨φ| sends |χ⟩ to |ψ⟩⟨φ|χ⟩. First compute the number ⟨1|χ⟩. |0⟩⟨1|χ⟩=|0⟩(3⟨1|0⟩+2i⟨1|1⟩)=2i|0⟩. The bra ⟨1| has no coefficient to conjugate, and the coefficient 2i of the ket stays as it is. 3|1⟩ is what |1⟩⟨0| gives, and 3|0⟩ is what |0⟩⟨0| gives. Every value of |0⟩⟨1| is a multiple of |0⟩, which is why an outer product has rank one.

Lecture 7 · Dirac notation, amplitudes, and what comes next

0 |0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthonormal, |ψ⟩=(√3/2)|0⟩+(i/2)|1⟩, and σ_z=|0⟩⟨0|−|1⟩⟨1|. What is the expectation value ⟨σ_z⟩=⟨ψ|σ_z|ψ⟩?

σ_z|ψ⟩=(√3/2)|0⟩−(i/2)|1⟩. Passing to the bra ⟨ψ| conjugates the coefficients.

Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. ⟨ψ|σ_z|ψ⟩=(√3/2)(√3/2)+(−i/2)(−i/2)=3/4−1/4=1/2. By the Born rule the values +1 and −1 are found with probabilities |√3/2|²=3/4 and |i/2|²=1/4, and their average is 3/4−1/4=1/2. Leaving i/2 unconjugated gives 3/4+1/4=1.

          Lecture 3 · The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps

          0 T: ℝ⁴→ℝ⁴ is linear and its image is a plane, dim im T=2. What is dim ker T?

          dim V = dim ker T + rank T.

          Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. dim ker T = 4 − rank T = 4 − 2 = 2.

          Lecture 3 · Kernel and injectivity

          0 A=[[1,2],[3,6]]. Which vector lies in im A?

          Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. The image is spanned by the columns; here they are parallel. Both columns are multiples of (1,3), so im A=span((1,3)), and (2,6)=2(1,3) is the only option on that line.

          Lecture 3 · Kernel and injectivity

          0 S,T∈ℒ(V) and ST=0. Which statement is always true?

          Here is the hint. Try once more — the explanation comes after that. Apply S to a vector of the form Tv. For every v, S(Tv)=0, so every vector of im T lies in ker S. The others can fail: S=[[0,1],[0,0]] and T=[[1,0],[0,0]] give ST=0 but TS≠0, though neither map is zero; and T=0, S=I give ker T=V but ker S={0}.

          0 R rotates ℝ² by 90° counterclockwise. What is the matrix of R² in the standard basis?
