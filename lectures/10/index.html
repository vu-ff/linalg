Lecture 9 showed that a normal operator has orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues. The spectral theorem completes the story in the most useful way possible: a normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex space has a complete orthonormal basis of eigenvectors—it is diagonal in an orthonormal basis, i.e. unitarily diagonalizable. For observables (self-adjoint operators) the eigenvalues are moreover real. This single theorem is the mathematical backbone of the finite ideal-projective model of quantum mechanics. It supplies the eigenspace projectors. The Born probabilities and the conditional Lueders update are separate physical postulates; the general Hilbert-space theory and generalized measurements are deferred to Lecture 14.

Schur’s theorem

The proof rests on triangularizing in an orthonormal basis.

Theorem 10.1 (Schur). Every operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an upper-triangular matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis.

Proof. By Lecture 5 (where triangularizability over C\CC is sketched), there is a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ in which TTT is upper-triangular, i.e. each subspace Uk=span⁡(v1,…,vk)U_k=\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)Uk​=span(v1​,…,vk​) is invariant, TUk⊆UkTU_k\subseteq U_kTUk​⊆Uk​. Gram–Schmidt (Lecture 8) produces an orthonormal e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with span⁡(e1,…,ek)=Uk\spn(e_1,\dots,e_k)=U_kspan(e1​,…,ek​)=Uk​ for every kkk, so the same subspaces stay invariant; in this orthonormal basis TTT is upper-triangular. 

Example 10.2 (Triangularizing in an orthonormal basis). A=(1011)A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)A=(11​01​) has the single eigenvalue 111 with eigenvector (0,1)(0,1)(0,1); taking the orthonormal basis e1=(0,1)e_1=(0,1)e1​=(0,1), e2=(1,0)e_2=(1,0)e2​=(1,0) gives M(A)=(1101)\Mat(A)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)M(A)=(10​11​), upper-triangular as Schur promises—but not diagonal, since AAA is not normal.

The complex spectral theorem

Theorem 10.3 (Complex spectral theorem). Suppose VVV is finite-dimensional over C\CC and T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V). The following are equivalent:

  1. TTT is normal;

  2. TTT has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis;

  3. VVV has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of TTT.

Proof. (2)⇔\Leftrightarrow⇔(3) is immediate, as the columns of a diagonal matrix in a basis say exactly that the basis vectors are eigenvectors. (2)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(1): a diagonal matrix and its conjugate transpose commute, so T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†.

(1)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(2): by Schur, take an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with M(T)=A\Mat(T)=AM(T)=A upper-triangular. From the first column, ∥Te1∥2=∣a11∣2\norm{Te_1}^2=\abs{a_{11}}^2∥Te1​∥2=∣a11​∣2, while M(T†)=A†\Mat(T^{\adj})=A^{\adj}M(T†)=A† gives, from its first column (the conjugated first row of AAA), ∥T†e1∥2=∣a11∣2+∣a12∣2+⋯+∣a1n∣2\norm{T^{\adj}e_1}^2=\abs{a_{11}}^2+\abs{a_{12}}^2+\cdots +\abs{a_{1n}}^2​T†e1​​2=∣a11​∣2+∣a12​∣2+⋯+∣a1n​∣2. Normality forces ∥Te1∥=∥T†e1∥\norm{Te_1}=\norm{T^{\adj}e_1}∥Te1​∥=​T†e1​​ (Lecture 9), so a12=⋯=a1n=0a_{12}=\cdots=a_{1n}=0a12​=⋯=a1n​=0. Hence A=a11⊕A1A=a_{11}\oplus A_1A=a11​⊕A1​. Comparing the two diagonal blocks of A†A=AA†A^{\adj}A=AA^{\adj}A†A=AA† gives A1†A1=A1A1†A_1^{\adj}A_1=A_1A_1^{\adj}A1†​A1​=A1​A1†​, so the trailing upper-triangular block A1A_1A1​ is normal. Induction on the dimension makes A1A_1A1​ diagonal; therefore AAA is diagonal. 

In matrix language: AAA is normal iff A=UDU†A=UDU^{\adj}A=UDU† with UUU unitary and DDD diagonal. The columns of UUU are the orthonormal eigenvectors; conjugating by the unitary UUU (a change of orthonormal basis, Lecture 9) reveals the diagonal DDD.

Example 10.7 (Circulant matrices and the Fourier basis). A circulant matrix—cyclically constant along diagonals, e.g. (abccabbca)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b&c\\c&a&b\\b&c&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)(acb​bac​cba​)—is normal, and every circulant is diagonalized by the one orthonormal Fourier basis fk=1n(1,ωk,ω2k,… )f_k=\tfrac1{\sqrt n}(1,\omega^k,\omega^{2k},\dots)fk​=n​1​(1,ωk,ω2k,…) with ω=e2πi/n\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}ω=e2πi/n. Its eigenvalues are the discrete Fourier transform of the first row. This is why the FFT diagonalizes convolution: a single unitary serves all circulants at once.

Remark 10.8 (The spectrum is a complete invariant). Two normal operators are unitarily equivalent if and only if they have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, since each is unitarily equivalent to the diagonal matrix of its eigenvalues. For normal operators the spectrum (with multiplicity) is therefore a complete invariant—unlike the non-normal case, where Jordan structure also matters.

Remark 10.9 (Bridge: commuting normal operators). A finite commuting family of normal operators on a finite-dimensional complex space can be simultaneously unitarily diagonalized. Diagonalize one operator. Every other operator BBB preserves each of its eigenspaces by commutation. Since their orthogonal direct sum is all of VVV, BBB is block diagonal in this decomposition, so B†B^{\adj}B† has the adjoint blocks; comparing the blocks in B†B=BB†B^{\adj}B=BB^{\adj}B†B=BB† shows that every restriction of BBB is normal. Diagonalize the restrictions. Each remaining operator preserves the resulting common eigenspaces, so the same block argument iterates through the finite family and produces one orthonormal basis of joint eigenvectors. The core measurement application below uses the self-adjoint pair case.

Remark 10.10 (Why normality is needed). Normality is not optional. The shear (1101)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)(10​11​) is not normal and has only a one-dimensional eigenspace, so no basis—let alone an orthonormal one—consists of its eigenvectors. The spectral theorem is precisely the statement that normal is the exact dividing line for unitary diagonalizability.

Self-adjoint operators and the real case

Theorem 10.11 (Spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators). A self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner product space (real or complex) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, with all eigenvalues real. Equivalently, a Hermitian matrix factors as A=UDU†A=UDU^{\adj}A=UDU† with UUU unitary and DDD real diagonal; a real symmetric matrix factors as A=ODOTA=ODO^{\T}A=ODOT with OOO orthogonal.

Proof. A self-adjoint operator is normal, so over C\CC the complex spectral theorem gives an orthonormal eigenbasis, and the eigenvalues are real by Lecture 9.

Over R\RR the argument needs one extra step, because Theorem 10.1 as stated covers complex spaces. Let AAA be the real symmetric matrix of TTT in an orthonormal basis and regard it for a moment as a complex Hermitian matrix. Its characteristic polynomial is the same either way, and by Lecture 9 all of its roots are real; hence χT\chi_TχT​ splits over R\RR. The proof of Theorem 10.1 uses nothing about C\CC beyond the fact that χT\chi_TχT​ splits—it triangularizes and then runs Gram–Schmidt—so it applies verbatim over R\RR and yields a real orthonormal basis in which TTT is upper-triangular. A real symmetric upper-triangular matrix equals its transpose, hence is diagonal: an orthogonal eigenbasis with real entries. 

For the finite-dimensional ideal-projective model this is the crucial statement: every self-adjoint observable has a complete orthonormal set of eigenstates with real eigenvalues. General observables with continuous spectrum need the projection-valued measures of Lecture 14. The real version is the “principal axes theorem”—a real symmetric matrix (inertia tensor, stress tensor, quadratic form) is diagonal in a suitable set of mutually perpendicular axes.

Spectral decomposition

Group the eigenvalues into the distinct values λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​, and let PkP_kPk​ be the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace E(λk,T)E(\lambda_k,T)E(λk​,T).

Theorem 10.19 (Spectral decomposition). A normal operator TTT on a finite-dimensional complex space (or a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional real space) with distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​ satisfies PjPk=δjkPk,P1+⋯+Pm=I,T=λ1P1+⋯+λmPm,P_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_k,\qquad P_1+\cdots+P_m=I,\qquad T=\lambda_1P_1+\cdots+\lambda_mP_m,Pj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​,P1​+⋯+Pm​=I,T=λ1​P1​+⋯+λm​Pm​, with each PkP_kPk​ self-adjoint (Pk†=PkP_k^{\adj}=P_kPk†​=Pk​). Consequently f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_k f(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​ for any function fff on the spectrum.

Proof. The eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal (Lecture 9) and, by the spectral theorem, span VVV, so V=E(λ1)⊕⋯⊕E(λm)V=E(\lambda_1)\dsum\cdots\dsum E(\lambda_m)V=E(λ1​)⊕⋯⊕E(λm​) orthogonally. The PkP_kPk​ are then orthogonal projections onto orthogonal summands, giving PjPk=δjkPkP_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_kPj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​ and ∑kPk=I\sum_kP_k=I∑k​Pk​=I. Applying both sides of the last equation to an eigenvector shows T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​, and orthogonal projections are self-adjoint. Applying fff eigenvalue by eigenvalue gives f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_k f(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​. 

In the same finite setting, with an orthonormal eigenbasis {∣ek⟩}\set{\ket{e_k}}{∣ek​⟩}, T=∑kλk∣ek⟩⟨ek∣,I=∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣:T=\sum_k\lambda_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k},\qquad I=\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}:T=k∑​λk​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣,I=k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣: the operator is a weighted sum of rank-one basis projectors, and the identity is their sum (the completeness relation of Lecture 8). When an eigenvalue is degenerate, its one physical ideal-projective outcome uses the higher-rank sum of all these basis projectors inside that eigenspace.

Example 10.29 (Inverse from the spectrum). A normal T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​ with every λk≠0\lambda_k\neq0λk​=0 is invertible, with T−1=∑kλk−1PkT^{-1}=\sum_k\lambda_k^{-1}P_kT−1=∑k​λk−1​Pk​: indeed (∑kλkPk)(∑jλj−1Pj)=∑kPk=I\bigl(\sum_k\lambda_kP_k\bigr)\bigl(\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1}P_j\bigr)=\sum_kP_k=I(∑k​λk​Pk​)(∑j​λj−1​Pj​)=∑k​Pk​=I, using PjPk=δjkPkP_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_kPj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​. No function of an operator asks more than the same function of its eigenvalues.

Remark 10.30 (The functional calculus). The map f↦f(T)f\mapsto f(T)f↦f(T) is an algebra homomorphism: (f+g)(T)=f(T)+g(T)(f+g)(T)=f(T)+g(T)(f+g)(T)=f(T)+g(T), (fg)(T)=f(T)g(T)(fg)(T)=f(T)g(T)(fg)(T)=f(T)g(T), and f‾(T)=f(T)†\conj f(T)=f(T)^{\adj}f​(T)=f(T)†. Because f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_kf(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​, each of these reduces to the corresponding fact about numbers applied eigenvalue by eigenvalue—the functional calculus of a normal operator.

Remark 10.31 (Trace and determinant from the spectrum). Because λk\lambda_kλk​ indexes distinct eigenvalues, tr⁡T=∑kλkrank⁡(Pk)\tr T=\sum_k\lambda_k\rank(P_k)trT=∑k​λk​rank(Pk​) and det⁡T=∏kλkrank⁡(Pk)\det T=\prod_k\lambda_k^{\rank(P_k)}detT=∏k​λkrank(Pk​)​, both basis-independent (Lecture 5). Thus diag⁡(2,2,5)\diag(2,2,5)diag(2,2,5) has trace 2⋅2+5=92\cdot2+5=92⋅2+5=9 and determinant 22⋅5=202^2\cdot5=2022⋅5=20. Equivalently rank⁡(Pk)=tr⁡Pk\rank(P_k)=\tr P_krank(Pk​)=trPk​, so the trace follows directly from the spectral decomposition.

Remark 10.32 (Extension: singular value decomposition). Even a non-normal TTT yields to a spectral idea: applying the theorem to the positive operator T†TT^{\adj}TT†T gives the singular value decomposition T=UΣV†T=U\Sigma V^{\adj}T=UΣV† with U,VU,VU,V unitary and Σ\SigmaΣ diagonal with nonnegative entries—the universal factorization behind low-rank approximation and the pseudoinverse.

Measurement and what comes next

The finite spectral theorem supplies the projectors used by the ideal projective measurement postulate. For a normalized pure-state representative, the separate Born postulate says that an observable A=∑kλkPkA=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kA=∑k​λk​Pk​ yields outcome λk\lambda_kλk​ with probability ∥Pk∣ψ⟩∥2\norm{P_k\ket\psi}^2∥Pk​∣ψ⟩∥2 (the Born rule), after which the state collapses to the normalized projection Pk∣ψ⟩/∥Pk∣ψ⟩∥P_k\ket\psi/\norm{P_k\ket\psi}Pk​∣ψ⟩/∥Pk​∣ψ⟩∥ when that probability is nonzero (the Lueders update postulate). Because ∑kPk=I\sum_kP_k=I∑k​Pk​=I and the PkP_kPk​ are orthogonal, these probabilities sum to one. Expectation values are ⟨A⟩=∑kλk∥Pk∣ψ⟩∥2\expval A=\sum_k\lambda_k\norm{P_k\ket\psi}^2⟨A⟩=∑k​λk​∥Pk​∣ψ⟩∥2.

Example 10.33 (A measurement). Measuring σz\sigma_zσz​ on ∣ψ⟩=15(∣0⟩+2∣1⟩)\ket\psi=\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(\ket0+2\ket1)∣ψ⟩=5​1​(∣0⟩+2∣1⟩): the outcome +1+1+1 occurs with probability ∥P+∣ψ⟩∥2=15\norm{P_+\ket\psi}^2=\tfrac15∥P+​∣ψ⟩∥2=51​ and −1-1−1 with probability 45\tfrac4554​, collapsing ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ to ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ or ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩ respectively. The expectation value is ⟨σz⟩=(+1)15+(−1)45=−35\expval{\sigma_z}=(+1)\tfrac15+(-1)\tfrac45=-\tfrac35⟨σz​⟩=(+1)51​+(−1)54​=−53​, the probability-weighted average of the eigenvalues.

Example 10.34 (Measuring a degenerate observable). For A=diag⁡(5,5,2)A=\diag(5,5,2)A=diag(5,5,2) on C3\C^3C3 the eigenvalue 555 has a two-dimensional eigenspace with projection P5=diag⁡(1,1,0)P_5=\diag(1,1,0)P5​=diag(1,1,0). Measuring AAA on ∣ψ⟩=(a,b,c)\ket\psi=(a,b,c)∣ψ⟩=(a,b,c) returns 555 with probability ∥P5ψ∥2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2\norm{P_5\psi}^2=\abs a^2+\abs b^2∥P5​ψ∥2=∣a∣2+∣b∣2, collapsing the state into the 555-eigenspace rather than to a single vector. Degenerate outcomes project onto whole eigenspaces.

Example 10.35 (Time evolution via the spectral form). For a finite-dimensional, time-independent Hamiltonian H^=∑nEnPn\hat H=\sum_n E_nP_nH^=∑n​En​Pn​, e−iH^t/ℏ=∑ne−iEnt/ℏPn,e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}=\sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}P_n,e−iH^t/ℏ=n∑​e−iEn​t/ℏPn​, so evolution acts on each energy eigenspace by a phase. A state ∣ψ⟩=∑nPn∣ψ⟩\ket\psi=\sum_nP_n\ket\psi∣ψ⟩=∑n​Pn​∣ψ⟩ becomes ∑ne−iEnt/ℏPn∣ψ⟩\sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}P_n\ket\psi∑n​e−iEn​t/ℏPn​∣ψ⟩, and the outcome probabilities ∥Pn∣ψ⟩∥2\norm{P_n\ket\psi}^2∥Pn​∣ψ⟩∥2 are conserved—energy conservation, read off the spectral decomposition.

Example 10.36 (Measuring in a rotated basis). Prepare ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ (a σz\sigma_zσz​-eigenstate) and measure σx\sigma_xσx​, whose eigenstates are ∣±⟩\ket\pm∣±⟩. The amplitudes ⟨+,0⟩=⟨−,0⟩=12\inner{+}{0}=\inner{-}{0}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}⟨+,0⟩=⟨−,0⟩=2​1​ give both outcomes ±1\pm1±1 probability 12\tfrac1221​: a state sharp in one observable is maximally uncertain in an incompatible one.

Remark 10.37 (Extension: Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures). One may evolve states by e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ or, equivalently, evolve observables by A(t)=eiH^t/ℏAe−iH^t/ℏA(t)=e^{i\hat Ht/\hbar}Ae^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}A(t)=eiH^t/ℏAe−iH^t/ℏ. Conjugation by a unitary preserves the spectrum, so A(t)A(t)A(t) has the same eigenvalues as AAA at all times: the possible outcomes never change, only their probabilities.

Example 10.38 (A three-outcome measurement). Measuring A=diag⁡(1,2,3)A=\diag(1,2,3)A=diag(1,2,3) on ∣ψ⟩=13(1,1,1)\ket\psi=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)∣ψ⟩=3​1​(1,1,1) gives each outcome 1,2,31,2,31,2,3 with probability 13\tfrac1331​, so ⟨A⟩=13(1+2+3)=2,⟨A2⟩=13(1+4+9)=143,\expval A=\tfrac13(1+2+3)=2,\qquad \expval{A^2}=\tfrac13(1+4+9)=\tfrac{14}3,⟨A⟩=31​(1+2+3)=2,⟨A2⟩=31​(1+4+9)=314​, and the variance is ⟨A2⟩−⟨A⟩2=143−4=23\expval{A^2}-\expval A^2=\tfrac{14}3-4=\tfrac23⟨A2⟩−⟨A⟩2=314​−4=32​. The spectral data deliver every statistical moment of a measurement.

Remark 10.39 (Repeated measurement). In the finite ideal-projective model, immediately repeating the same measurement returns the same outcome with certainty: the first Lueders update leaves the state in an eigenspace of AAA, on which AAA acts as a single eigenvalue. This is an implication of that physical update postulate, not of the spectral theorem alone.

Commuting finite-dimensional self-adjoint observables can be diagonalized simultaneously in one orthonormal basis, so a maximal set of commuting observables (a “complete set of commuting observables”) labels states by joint eigenvalues—the quantum numbers of a system. Non-commuting observables share no eigenbasis, and the uncertainty relation of Lecture 9 measures how badly. The next lectures leave finite dimensions behind: dual spaces and tensors (Lectures 11–12), then Hilbert spaces and the spectral theorem for operators with continuous spectra (Lectures 13–14), where position and momentum finally find their home.

Example 10.40 (Extension: a complete set of commuting observables). On C3\C^3C3, A=diag⁡(1,1,2)A=\diag(1,1,2)A=diag(1,1,2) and B=diag⁡(3,5,5)B=\diag(3,5,5)B=diag(3,5,5) commute and share the standard orthonormal eigenbasis. Neither alone separates all three states—AAA cannot tell e1e_1e1​ from e2e_2e2​, BBB cannot tell e2e_2e2​ from e3e_3e3​—but the joint eigenvalues (1,3),(1,5),(2,5)(1,3),(1,5),(2,5)(1,3),(1,5),(2,5) are distinct, so together A,BA,BA,B label the states completely, a finite model of a complete set of commuting observables.

Example 10.41 (A shared eigenbasis). A=σx=(0110)A=\sigma_x=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)A=σx​=(01​10​) and B=(2112)B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)B=(21​12​) commute, since AB=BA=(1221)AB=BA=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)AB=BA=(12​21​), so they share the orthonormal eigenbasis ∣±⟩=12(1,±1)\ket\pm=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,\pm1)∣±⟩=2​1​(1,±1): there A=diag⁡(1,−1)A=\diag(1,-1)A=diag(1,−1) and B=diag⁡(3,1)B=\diag(3,1)B=diag(3,1). Commuting observables diagonalize together even when neither is diagonal to begin with.

Remark 10.42 (Extension: density matrices). A statistical mixture of states is a density matrix ρ=∑kpk∣ψk⟩⟨ψk∣\rho=\sum_k p_k\ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k}ρ=∑k​pk​∣ψk​⟩⟨ψk​∣, a positive self-adjoint operator with tr⁡ρ=1\tr\rho=1trρ=1. By the spectral theorem ρ\rhoρ has an orthonormal eigenbasis with nonnegative eigenvalues summing to one—its own probability distribution over orthogonal pure states. The spectral theorem thus governs mixed states as well as pure ones.

Remark 10.43 (Beyond finite dimensions). The position operator has no eigenvectors in the usual sense: its spectrum is the continuous range of possible positions. The finite sum A=∑kλkPkA=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kA=∑k​λk​Pk​ then becomes an integral ∫λ dP(λ)\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)∫λdP(λ) against a projection-valued measure—the spectral theorem of Lecture 14, for which the finite-dimensional version proved here is the template.

The finite spectral theorem says a normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors—equivalently a diagonal matrix in an orthonormal basis, T=UDU†T=UDU^{\adj}T=UDU† with UUU unitary. Self-adjoint operators are the special case with real eigenvalues (and a real orthogonal eigenbasis when the space is real), the principal-axes theorem. Grouping eigenspaces gives the spectral decomposition T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​ with orthogonal projections satisfying PjPk=δjkPkP_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_kPj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​ and ∑kPk=I\sum_kP_k=I∑k​Pk​=I, whence f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_kf(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​. In the finite quantum model this supplies real spectral values and eigenspace projectors. The separate Born and Lueders postulates give ideal-projective probabilities and conditional updates, and commuting observables diagonalize together. Continuous spectra and generalized measurements are deferred to Lecture 14.

  • Schur’s theorem—every finite-dimensional complex operator is upper-triangular in some orthonormal basis.
  • Complex spectral theorem—in finite dimension, normal   ⟺  \iff⟺ orthonormal eigenbasis   ⟺  \iff⟺ unitarily diagonalizable (T=UDU†T=UDU^{\adj}T=UDU†).
  • Real spectral theorem—in finite dimension, self-adjoint gives a real orthonormal eigenbasis (principal axes) and real eigenvalues.
  • Spectral decomposition—the finite sum T=∑kλkPkT=\sum_k\lambda_kP_kT=∑k​λk​Pk​ has PjPk=δjkPkP_jP_k=\delta_{jk}P_kPj​Pk​=δjk​Pk​, ∑kPk=I\sum_kP_k=I∑k​Pk​=I; f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_kf(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​.
  • Finite projective model—the theorem supplies projectors; Born probabilities and the Lueders update are separate postulates; commuting observables diagonalize together.

Self-check

6 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1Which class of operators on a finite-dimensional complex space has an orthonormal eigenbasis?

This is the hypothesis of the complex spectral theorem.

The complex spectral theorem says: normal if and only if unitarily diagonalisable.

2If Tv=λv, what does the operator f(T) do to v?

A function of an operator acts separately along each eigendirection.

Spectral mapping replaces λ by f(λ) while preserving the eigendirection.

3The state is ψ=(3/5)e₁+(4/5)e₂. What is the probability of measuring the eigenvalue associated with e₂?

The probability is the squared modulus of the amplitude.

|4/5|²=16/25=0.64.

4A finite-dimensional self-adjoint operator has…

Apply the self-adjoint spectral theorem.

Self-adjoint operators are unitarily diagonalizable and their eigenvalues are real.

5For spectral projections Pⱼ and a unit state ψ, why do the probabilities ‖Pⱼψ‖² sum to one?

Use orthogonal decomposition and Pythagoras.

ψ=ΣPⱼψ is an orthogonal decomposition, so Σ‖Pⱼψ‖²=‖ψ‖²=1.

6What follows when two finite-dimensional self-adjoint operators commute?

Restrict one observable to each eigenspace of the other.

Commuting finite-dimensional self-adjoint operators can be diagonalized simultaneously in one orthonormal basis.
