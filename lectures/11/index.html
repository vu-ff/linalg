We have studied vectors and the operators that move them. This lecture studies the measurements one can make on vectors: the linear functionals φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F, which assemble into the dual space V∗V^*V∗. In an inner product space the Riesz theorem of Lecture 8 already tied functionals to vectors—a bra ⟨u∣\bra u⟨u∣ is a dual vector. Here we build the dual abstractly, without an inner product: dual bases, the dual map (the transpose, freed from coordinates), bilinear and multilinear forms, and the covariant/contravariant bookkeeping with Einstein’s summation convention that runs through tensor calculus and relativity. This is Part II of the course: the multilinear algebra underneath tensors.

Remark 11.1 (Standing scope for Lectures 11–12). The core is finite-dimensional over R\RR or C\CC. Here V∗=L(V,F)V^*=\Lin(V,\F)V∗=L(V,F) is the algebraic dual. In a complex inner-product space the physics-convention Riesz map V→V∗V\to V^*V→V∗ is conjugate-linear (or linear from the conjugate space); the continuous Hilbert dual is introduced in Lecture 13.

The dual space

Definition 11.2 (Dual space). A linear functional on VVV is a linear map φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F. The set of all linear functionals, with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication, is the dual space V∗=L(V,F)V^*=\Lin(V,\F)V∗=L(V,F).

On P(F)\Poly(\F)P(F), p↦p(2)p\mapsto p(2)p↦p(2) evaluates and p↦∫01pp\mapsto\int_0^1 pp↦∫01​p integrates. On Mn(F)\Mat_n(\F)Mn​(F), A↦tr⁡AA\mapsto\tr AA↦trA. On an inner product space, v↦⟨u,v⟩v\mapsto\inner u vv↦⟨u,v⟩ for fixed uuu. All are linear, so all live in V∗V^*V∗.

Remark 11.4 (Invariant functionals). The trace is special among functionals on Mn\Mat_nMn​: it is invariant under similarity, tr⁡(P−1AP)=tr⁡A\tr(P^{-1}AP)=\tr Atr(P−1AP)=trA, so it descends to a functional on operators, not just matrices. Most functionals depend on the basis; the few that do not encode genuine invariants.

Proposition 11.5 (Dimension of the dual). If dim⁡V=n\dim V=ndimV=n then dim⁡V∗=n\dim V^*=ndimV∗=n, so V∗≅VV^*\iso VV∗≅V (non-canonically).

Proof. Fix a basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ of VVV. A linear functional is determined by its values on the eje_jej​, and those nnn values may be assigned independently, so φ↦(φ(e1),…,φ(en))\varphi\mapsto(\varphi(e_1),\dots,\varphi(e_n))φ↦(φ(e1​),…,φ(en​)) is an isomorphism V∗≅FnV^*\iso\F^nV∗≅Fn. Hence dim⁡V∗=n\dim V^*=ndimV∗=n. 

The isomorphism with VVV is not canonical—it depends on a choice of basis—which is the whole point of treating V∗V^*V∗ as a space in its own right.

Functionals, the Riesz map, and the double dual

A nonzero functional φ\varphiφ has null⁡φ\nul\varphinullφ of dimension n−1n-1n−1: its level sets are parallel hyperplanes, and φ(v)\varphi(v)φ(v) counts (with sign and scale) how many it takes to reach vvv. This is the geometric picture of a covector.

Proposition 11.13 (Finite Riesz comparison). Let VVV be a finite-dimensional inner-product space over F\FF. Every algebraic functional φ∈V∗\varphi\in V^*φ∈V∗ has a unique representative u∈Vu\in Vu∈V with φ(v)=⟨u,v⟩\varphi(v)=\inner u vφ(v)=⟨u,v⟩ for all vvv. The map R ⁣:u↦⟨u, ⋅ ⟩R\colon u\mapsto\inner u{\,\cdot\,}R:u↦⟨u,⋅⟩ is linear over R\RR and conjugate-linear over C\CC; equivalently, in the complex case it is a linear isomorphism V‾→V∗\overline V\to V^*V→V∗ from the conjugate vector space.

This is the finite theorem proved in Lecture 8. In Dirac notation R(u)=⟨u∣R(u)=\bra uR(u)=⟨u∣, so a bra acts on ∣v⟩\ket v∣v⟩ to give ⟨u | v⟩\braket u v⟨u∣v⟩. It must not be confused with the canonical double-dual map below: RRR needs an inner product and is conjugate-linear over C\CC, whereas J ⁣:V→V∗∗J\colon V\to V^{**}J:V→V∗∗ needs no metric and is linear over both fields.

Remark 11.16 (Algebraic versus continuous dual). The proposition is deliberately finite. On c00c_{00}c00​ with the ℓ2\ell^2ℓ2 inner product, the algebraic functional φ(x)=∑nxn\varphi(x)=\sum_nx_nφ(x)=∑n​xn​ is well-defined but unbounded and cannot be ⟨u, ⋅ ⟩\inner u{\,\cdot\,}⟨u,⋅⟩ for any finitely supported uuu. In infinite-dimensional algebraic spaces V∗V^*V∗ can be much larger than VVV, and the canonical map V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ below need not be onto.

Theorem 11.17 (The double dual). For finite-dimensional VVV, the map V→V∗∗V\to V^{**}V→V∗∗ sending vvv to the functional φ↦φ(v)\varphi\mapsto\varphi(v)φ↦φ(v) is a canonical isomorphism (no basis required).

So VVV and V∗∗V^{**}V∗∗ may be identified outright: a vector is equally a thing functionals act on, and a thing that acts on functionals.

Remark 11.19 (Bridge: the dual space in quantum mechanics). In the finite model, kets ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ live in VVV and bras ⟨ψ∣\bra\psi⟨ψ∣ live in V∗V^*V∗. In a Hilbert space a bra belongs to the continuous dual, not generally the full algebraic dual. A matrix element ⟨ϕ∣ A ∣ψ⟩\matrixel{\phi}{A}{\psi}⟨ϕ∣A∣ψ⟩ pairs the bra ⟨ϕ∣\bra\phi⟨ϕ∣ (a functional) with the vector A∣ψ⟩A\ket\psiA∣ψ⟩. The whole bra–ket formalism says that physical amplitudes are functionals evaluated on states.

The dual map

Definition 11.20 (Dual map). For T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W), the dual map T′∈L(W∗,V∗)T'\in\Lin(W^*,V^*)T′∈L(W∗,V∗) is T′φ=φ∘TT'\varphi=\varphi\circ TT′φ=φ∘T, i.e. (T′φ)(v)=φ(Tv)(T'\varphi)(v)=\varphi(Tv)(T′φ)(v)=φ(Tv).

The dual map pulls a measurement on WWW back to a measurement on VVV. It reverses arrows—T ⁣:V→WT\colon V\to WT:V→W gives T′ ⁣:W∗→V∗T'\colon W^*\to V^*T′:W∗→V∗—and satisfies (ST)′=T′S′(ST)'=T'S'(ST)′=T′S′ and (S+T)′=S′+T′(S+T)'=S'+T'(S+T)′=S′+T′.

Proof. Let e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ and f1,…,fmf_1,\dots,f_mf1​,…,fm​ be the bases of VVV and WWW, with dual bases eie^iei and faf^afa, and write Tei=∑aMai faTe_i=\sum_a M^a{}_i\,f_aTei​=∑a​Mai​fa​, so M=M(T)M=\Mat(T)M=M(T). Bilinear and multilinear forms

Definition 11.31 (Bilinear form). A bilinear form is a map B ⁣:V×V→FB\colon V\times V\to\FB:V×V→F linear in each argument. In a basis its matrix is Bij=B(ei,ej)B_{ij}=B(e_i,e_j)Bij​=B(ei​,ej​), and B(u,v)=∑i,juiBijvj=uTBvB(u,v)=\sum_{i,j}u^i B_{ij}v^j=u^{\T}BvB(u,v)=∑i,j​uiBij​vj=uTBv.

A bilinear form is symmetric if B(u,v)=B(v,u)B(u,v)=B(v,u)B(u,v)=B(v,u) (matrix BT=BB^{\T}=BBT=B) and alternating if B(v,v)=0B(v,v)=0B(v,v)=0 (matrix antisymmetric). A symmetric bilinear form is essentially a (real) inner product when it is also positive definite; the quadratic form Q(v)=B(v,v)Q(v)=B(v,v)Q(v)=B(v,v) recovers it by polarization.

Remark 11.32 (Bilinear versus sesquilinear). Over C\CC the physics inner product is not bilinear but sesquilinear: conjugate-linear in the first slot (Lecture 7). A true bilinear form satisfies B(λu,v)=λB(u,v)B(\lambda u,v)=\lambda B(u,v)B(λu,v)=λB(u,v) with no conjugate, so the inner product is a slightly different animal—and it is exactly this conjugation that makes the Riesz identification V∗≅VV^*\iso VV∗≅V conjugate-linear over C\CC.

Definition 11.33 (Multilinear form). A kkk-linear form is a map V×⋯×V→FV\times\cdots\times V\to\FV×⋯×V→F (kkk factors) linear in each slot separately. The determinant is the unique alternating nnn-linear form on Fn\F^nFn with det⁡(e1,…,en)=1\det(e_1,\dots,e_n)=1det(e1​,…,en​)=1 (see the determinant facts recalled in Lecture 5; Example 11.36 below verifies the 2×22\times22×2 case).

Example 11.36 (The determinant as a form). On F2\F^2F2 the determinant D(u,v)=u1v2−u2v1D(u,v)=u_1v_2-u_2v_1D(u,v)=u1​v2​−u2​v1​ is bilinear, alternating (D(v,v)=0D(v,v)=0D(v,v)=0), and normalized (D(e1,e2)=1D(e_1,e_2)=1D(e1​,e2​)=1). Example 11.37 (Quadratic form and polarization). The symmetric form B(u,v)=u1v1+u2v2+u1v2+u2v1B(u,v)=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_1v_2+u_2v_1B(u,v)=u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u1​v2​+u2​v1​ has quadratic form Q(v)=B(v,v)=v12+v22+2v1v2=(v1+v2)2Q(v)=B(v,v)=v_1^2+v_2^2+2v_1v_2=(v_1+v_2)^2Q(v)=B(v,v)=v12​+v22​+2v1​v2​=(v1​+v2​)2. Example 11.38 (Signature of a form). For a nondegenerate real symmetric form, the two-entry signature (p,q)(p,q)(p,q) records its positive and negative counts and is invariant under change of basis (Sylvester’s law of inertia). A genuine inner product has signature (n,0)(n,0)(n,0); the Minkowski pseudo-metric below has signature (1,3)(1,3)(1,3). Degenerate forms require a third zero count.

Example 11.39 (A volume form). On R3\R^3R3 the determinant D(u,v,w)=det⁡[u v w]D(u,v,w)=\det[u\ v\ w]D(u,v,w)=det[u v w] is an alternating 333-linear form giving the signed volume of the parallelepiped on u,v,wu,v,wu,v,w. Alternation encodes orientation: swapping two arguments flips the sign, and a repeated argument gives zero—a degenerate, volumeless box.

Example 11.40 (An antisymmetric form). On R2\R^2R2 the form ω(u,v)=u1v2−u2v1\omega(u,v)=u_1v_2-u_2v_1ω(u,v)=u1​v2​−u2​v1​ is alternating, with matrix (01−10)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)(0−1​10​). Example 11.41 (The Gram matrix of an inner product). For vectors v1,…,vkv_1,\dots,v_kv1​,…,vk​ the Gram matrix Gij=⟨vi,vj⟩G_{ij}=\inner{v_i}{v_j}Gij​=⟨vi​,vj​⟩ is the matrix of the inner product restricted to their span. Over R\RR the inner product is bilinear and GGG is symmetric; over C\CC it is sesquilinear and GGG is Hermitian. In either case GGG is positive semidefinite, and det⁡G≠0\det G\neq0detG=0 exactly when the viv_ivi​ are linearly independent (Lecture 8).

Remark 11.42 (Extension: covectors as differential forms). A smoothly varying covector field is a differential 111-form, such as dfdfdf or the work integrand Fi dxiF_i\,dx^iFi​dxi. Integration pairs forms with curves, surfaces, and volumes, and the alternating multilinear forms of this lecture are the pointwise values of higher differential forms—the algebra beneath vector calculus and Stokes’ theorem.

Covariant and contravariant components

Choose a basis and write a vector by its components viv^ivi (upper index) and a covector by its components φi\varphi_iφi​ (lower index). Under a change of basis ei′=∑jPji eje_i'=\sum_j P^j{}_i\,e_jei′​=∑j​Pji​ej​ the two transform oppositely: vector components by [v]′=P−1[v][v]'=P^{-1}[v][v]′=P−1[v], while a fixed row covector has [φ]′=[φ]P[\varphi]'=[\varphi]P[φ]′=[φ]P (equivalently its component column obeys [φ]′=PT[φ][\varphi]'=P^{\T}[\varphi][φ]′=PT[φ]). By contrast, the rows representing the new dual-basis elements in the old coordinates form P−1P^{-1}P−1. These are different questions: the basis elements and the components of one fixed covector must not be conflated. Components transforming opposite to the basis are called contravariant; covector components are covariant.

Remark 11.43 (Einstein summation). A repeated index appearing once up and once down is summed: φ(v)=φivi\varphi(v)=\varphi_i v^iφ(v)=φi​vi means ∑iφivi\sum_i\varphi_i v^i∑i​φi​vi. The convention enforces a discipline—only an upper paired with a lower contracts to a scalar—that keeps expressions basis-independent and is the working language of relativity.

In a real inner-product space the positive-definite metric gij=⟨ei,ej⟩g_{ij}=\inner{e_i}{e_j}gij​=⟨ei​,ej​⟩ converts between the two: φi=gijvj\varphi_i=g_{ij}v^jφi​=gij​vj lowers an index (turning a vector into the covector that is its inner product), and the inverse metric gijg^{ij}gij raises it. This is exactly the Riesz identification written in components, and it is why physics distinguishes the contravariant 4-vector xμx^\muxμ from the covariant xμ=gμνxνx_\mu=g_{\mu\nu}x^\nuxμ​=gμν​xν. Throughout this section the inner product is taken real (the metric of tensor calculus and relativity), so gij=gjig_{ij}=g_{ji}gij​=gji​ and ⟨v,v⟩=gijvivj\inner v v=g_{ij}v^iv^j⟨v,v⟩=gij​vivj with no conjugation; over C\CC the metric is Hermitian, gij=gji‾g_{ij}=\conj{g_{ji}}gij​=gji​​, and index-lowering carries the conjugation of the conjugate-linear Riesz map of §3 (e.g. ⟨v,v⟩=vi‾ gijvj\inner v v=\conj{v^i}\,g_{ij}v^j⟨v,v⟩=vigij​vj and g′=P†gPg'=P^{\adj}gPg′=P†gP). More generally, raising and lowering over R\RR needs only a nondegenerate symmetric pseudo-metric; positivity is not required. The Minkowski form below is of this indefinite kind and is not an inner product.

Example 11.44 (Components transform oppositely). Rescale a basis vector to e1′=2e1e_1'=2e_1e1′​=2e1​. Example 11.48 (Bridge: raising and lowering with a pseudo-metric). With the Minkowski metric gμν=diag⁡(1,−1,−1,−1)g_{\mu\nu}=\diag(1,-1,-1,-1)gμν​=diag(1,−1,−1,−1), a contravariant 4-vector xμ=(t,x,y,z)x^\mu=(t,x,y,z)xμ=(t,x,y,z) has covariant form xμ=gμνxν=(t,−x,−y,−z),xμxμ=t2−x2−y2−z2,x_\mu=g_{\mu\nu}x^\nu=(t,-x,-y,-z),\qquad x_\mu x^\mu=t^2-x^2-y^2-z^2 ,xμ​=gμν​xν=(t,−x,−y,−z),xμ​xμ=t2−x2−y2−z2, the last being the invariant interval. Example 11.49 (Bridge: the differential and gradient). The differential dfdfdf eats a vector vvv and returns the directional derivative df(v)=∑i∂if vidf(v)=\sum_i\partial_if\,v^idf(v)=∑i​∂i​fvi, so its components ∂if\partial_if∂i​f carry a lower index: a gradient is naturally a covector. Remark 11.54 (Bridge: momentum is a covector). In mechanics the momentum pi=∂L/∂q˙ip_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q^ipi​=∂L/∂q˙​i carries a lower index: it is dual to the velocity q˙i\dot q^iq˙​i, and the pairing piq˙ip_i\dot q^ipi​q˙​i is the invariant Legendre scalar with energy units in H=piq˙i−LH=p_i\dot q^i-LH=pi​q˙​i−L. The distinction, invisible in an orthonormal Cartesian frame, becomes essential in curved or relativistic settings.

Remark 11.55 (Towards tensors). A vector carries one upper index, a covector one lower, a bilinear form two lower, a linear operator one of each. All are tensors, classified by how many indices of each type they carry and how those indices transform. Lecture 12 builds them systematically from tensor products.
