Operators on Hilbert Space

This final lecture carries the operator theory of Part I—adjoints, the spectral theorem, the operator classes of Lecture 9—into the infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces of Lecture 13, where it becomes the mathematical language of quantum mechanics. Two things change in infinite dimensions. First, eigenvalues no longer tell the whole story: an operator can be “non-invertible in the limit” without having any eigenvector, giving a continuous spectrum. Second, the operators of physics—position, momentum, energy—are unbounded, defined only on dense domains, and treating them rigorously is essential. We build operator norms and adjoints, classify the spectrum, meet position and momentum and their canonical commutation relation, and state the spectral theorem in the form quantum mechanics needs. The course closes with the dictionary between this linear algebra and the postulates of quantum theory.

Remark 14.1 (Standing scope for Lecture 14). Throughout, H\HilH is a complex Hilbert space and B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H) denotes its bounded operators. The algebraic notation L(V,W)\Lin(V,W)L(V,W) retains its earlier meaning. An unbounded operator is a linear expression together with a specified dense domain; adjoints, products, commutators, and functions of such operators retain domain conditions.

Remark 14.2 (Core, bridge, and extension route). The assessable core is B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H), adjoints, the three spectral parts, one shift, direct unboundedness, common-domain CCR and trace obstruction, boundary conditions, the qualified compact theorem, a domain-aware PVM theorem, and the pure/projective/closed quantum dictionary. Resolvents, compact-resolvent physics, Stone’s theorem, multiplication/free-particle models, and finite ideal measurement calculations are bridges. Repeated ladder algebra, kernel proofs, squeezed/coherent vistas, and general measurement/open-system developments are extensions.

The spectrum

Definition 14.10 (Spectrum). For T∈B(H)T\in\Bounded(\Hil)T∈B(H), the spectrum is spec⁡(T)={λ∈C:T−λid⁡ has no bounded everywhere inverse in B(H)}.\spec(T)=\set{\lambda\in\C : T-\lambda\idmap \text{ has no bounded everywhere inverse in }\Bounded(\Hil)}.spec(T)={λ∈C:T−λid has no bounded everywhere inverse in B(H)}. Writing A=T−λid⁡A=T-\lambda\idmapA=T−λid, the disjoint cases are: point spectrum when null⁡A≠{0}\nul A\ne\set0nullA={0}; continuous spectrum when AAA is injective with dense range but no bounded everywhere inverse; and residual spectrum when AAA is injective with non-dense range.

In finite dimensions “not invertible” means “has a kernel,” so the spectrum is exactly the eigenvalues. In infinite dimensions an injective operator may have dense nonclosed range (continuous spectrum) or non-dense range (residual spectrum). For a self-adjoint operator the spectrum is real; for a unitary operator it lies on the unit circle—the infinite-dimensional shadows of Lecture 9.

Remark 14.15 (Residual spectrum and normal operators). For completeness, the spectrum splits into three parts. If T−λid⁡T-\lambda\idmapT−λid has a kernel, λ\lambdaλ is in the point spectrum; if it is injective with dense range but no bounded inverse, λ\lambdaλ is in the continuous spectrum; and if it is injective with range that is not even dense, λ\lambdaλ is in the residual spectrum. The right shift exhibits the third kind: λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 is residual, since SSS is injective but its range misses e1e_1e1​ by a whole dimension. For normal operators—in particular every self-adjoint observable and every unitary—the residual spectrum is empty: if the range of T−λid⁡T-\lambda\idmapT−λid were not dense, its orthogonal complement null⁡(T†−λ‾id⁡)\nul(T^{\adj}-\conj\lambda\idmap)null(T†−λid) would be nonzero, and for a normal operator that forces null⁡(T−λid⁡)≠{0}\nul(T-\lambda\idmap)\neq\set0null(T−λid)={0} (Lecture 9), putting λ\lambdaλ in the point spectrum instead. Remark 14.27 (Domains matter). For unbounded operators, ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{Tu}{v}=\inner u{Tv}⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩ on a domain makes TTT symmetric, but self-adjoint additionally requires the adjoint to share that domain. Compact operators and the discrete spectral theorem

The closest infinite-dimensional analogue of a Hermitian matrix is a compact self-adjoint operator.

Definition 14.28 (Compact operator). An operator T∈B(H)T\in\Bounded(\Hil)T∈B(H) is compact if it is a norm-limit of finite-rank operators: there are operators TNT_NTN​ with finite-dimensional range and ∥T−TN∥→0\norm{T-T_N}\to0∥T−TN​∥→0. Equivalently, TTT maps the unit ball to a set in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence—TTT repairs, on its image, exactly the compactness failure of Lecture 13.

A diagonal operator Den=dnenDe_n=d_ne_nDen​=dn​en​ is compact iff dn→0d_n\to0dn​→0 (truncate to the first NNN entries: ∥D−DN∥=sup⁡n>N∣dn∣→0\norm{D-D_N}=\sup_{n>N}\abs{d_n}\to0∥D−DN​∥=supn>N​∣dn​∣→0), and the identity is not compact—the unit ball itself is not compact, as the orthonormal sequence of Lecture 13 showed. Compactness is thus a strong form of “finite-dimensional up to small corrections,” and it buys back the full finite-dimensional spectral theorem.

Theorem 14.29 (Spectral theorem, compact self-adjoint case). A compact self-adjoint operator TTT has a finite or countable set of nonzero real eigenvalues; each has finite multiplicity, and zero is their only possible accumulation point. Moreover H=null⁡T⊕range⁡T‾,\Hil=\nul T\dsum\overline{\rng T},H=nullT⊕rangeT​, the nonzero eigenspaces span range⁡T‾\overline{\rng T}rangeT​, and any orthonormal basis of null⁡T\nul TnullT completes them to an eigenbasis of H\HilH. In a separable infinite-dimensional space this basis may be listed as (en)(e_n)(en​) with eigenvalues λn→0\lambda_n\to0λn​→0 (including zero entries from the kernel), giving T=∑nλn∣en⟩⟨en∣T=\sum_n\lambda_n\ketbra{e_n}{e_n}T=∑n​λn​∣en​⟩⟨en​∣. In finite dimension the corresponding list and sum are finite; no limiting assertion is needed.

This is Lecture 10’s spectral decomposition with the kernel and separability qualifications visible. Standard confined Hamiltonians such as the box and oscillator are not compact—they are unbounded self-adjoint operators—but their resolvents are compact, which yields a discrete set of energy levels and an orthonormal basis of bound states.

The spectral theorem and the postulates of quantum mechanics

For a general self-adjoint operator—bounded or unbounded—the universal form is a PVM integral. Whether it reduces to an eigenprojection sum is determined by the atomic or non-atomic character of the spectral measure, not merely by which points lie in the operator-theoretic continuous spectrum.

Theorem 14.38 (Spectral theorem, general self-adjoint case). Every self-adjoint operator AAA has a projection-valued measure PPP on its spectrum with A=∫spec⁡(A)λ dP(λ).A=\int_{\spec(A)}\lambda\,dP(\lambda).A=∫spec(A)​λdP(λ). Writing μψ(Ω)=⟨ψ,P(Ω)ψ⟩\mu_\psi(\Omega)=\inner\psi{P(\Omega)\psi}μψ​(Ω)=⟨ψ,P(Ω)ψ⟩, an unbounded AAA acts on the natural domain D(A)={ψ∈H:∫spec⁡(A)λ2 dμψ(λ)<∞}.D(A)=\set{\psi\in\Hil:\int_{\spec(A)}\lambda^2\,d\mu_\psi(\lambda)<\infty}.D(A)={ψ∈H:∫spec(A)​λ2dμψ​(λ)<∞}. For a pure-point spectral measure the integral reduces to the corresponding eigenprojection sum. A non-atomic spectral component, as for multiplication by xxx on L2[−1,1]L^2[-1,1]L2[−1,1], requires a genuinely non-discrete integral. By contrast, Den=en/nDe_n=e_n/nDen​=en​/n has 000 in continuous spectrum but a pure-point PVM on the eigenvalues 1/n1/n1/n, with no eigenprojection at 000. Remark 14.53 (The layered quantum dictionary). The core model uses a normalized vector as a representative of a pure-state ray, a self-adjoint observable, an ideal PVM/Lueders measurement instrument, and a strongly continuous unitary group for closed-system dynamics. The general replacements are density operators for states, POVMs together with instruments for measurements, and quantum channels for open dynamics. Finite-level systems are genuine quantum mechanics; infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is needed for continuous canonical degrees of freedom and scattering.

Remark 14.54 (Why infinite dimensions). The trace obstruction of Proposition 14.24 is no technicality: the canonical commutation relation, the continuous spectra of position and momentum, and the scattering states of the free particle all require an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. This does not exclude finite-level quantum systems such as spin and qubits; it identifies the extra scope required by continuous canonical degrees of freedom.

On a complex H\HilH, bounded operators have adjoints and point, continuous, and residual spectrum. Domains govern unbounded position and momentum; their exact CCR has no finite-matrix model. Compact self-adjoint operators have a kernel-completed eigenbasis and nonzero spectrum accumulating only at zero. General self-adjoint operators use domain-aware PVMs, and strongly continuous dynamics use Stone generators. The pure/projective/closed QM model sits inside density-operator, POVM/instrument, and channel formulations.

  • Bounded operator—finite operator norm === continuity; adjoint via Riesz; the self-adjoint, unitary, and normal classes persist.
  • Spectrum—failure of bounded invertibility in B(H)\Bounded(\Hil)B(H); point, continuous (dense range), and residual (non-dense range), with no residual spectrum for normal operators.
  • Unbounded observables—x^\hat xx^ and p^=−iℏ d/dx\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dxp^​=−iℏd/dx obey [x^,p^]=iℏid⁡[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\idmap[x^,p^​]=iℏid, impossible in finite dimensions (a trace argument).
  • Compact self-adjoint—nonzero eigenvalues have finite multiplicity and only accumulate at zero; kernel plus nonzero eigenspaces gives an eigenbasis.
  • General spectral theorem—A=∫λ dP(λ)A=\int\lambda\,dP(\lambda)A=∫λdP(λ) on its natural domain; pure-point PVMs reduce this to eigenprojection sums, while non-atomic components do not; strongly continuous unitary groups have self-adjoint Stone generators.
  • QM dictionary—pure/PVM/closed is the core model; density operators, POVMs with instruments, and channels are the general replacements.

Self-check

10 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1A linear operator T is bounded when…

The operator norm is a uniform ratio bound.

Boundedness is equivalent to a global constant C controlling output norm by input norm.

2When does λ belong to the spectrum σ(T)?

Use the invertibility definition; eigenvalues are only one way it can fail.

The spectrum consists of all λ for which T−λI has no bounded inverse.

3In infinite dimensions, can a spectral value fail to be an eigenvalue?

Invertibility can fail even when the kernel is zero.

In the continuous spectrum, T−λI is injective with dense, non-surjective range; in the residual spectrum its range is not dense. Neither case requires an eigenvector.

4If T−λI is injective but its range is not dense, which spectral part contains λ?

Density of the range separates the continuous and residual cases.

Injectivity rules out point spectrum, while non-dense range is the defining residual-spectrum obstruction.

5What is the canonical commutation relation for position x and momentum p?

This relation drives the position–momentum uncertainty principle.

On a suitable dense domain, xp−px=iℏI.

6For an unbounded observable, which data are part of the operator?

The same differential expression can define different operators.

An unbounded operator is specified by both a rule and a domain; symmetry and self-adjointness also depend on that domain.

7For a compact self-adjoint operator, non-zero eigenvalues can accumulate only at…

Compact spectra resemble a sequence shrinking toward one special point.

The spectral theorem gives real eigenvalues tending to zero, with finite multiplicity away from zero.

8A normalised state is (1/2)|0⟩+(√3/2)|1⟩. For an observable whose outcome label 1 has projector |1⟩⟨1|, what is the probability of outcome 1?

Square the modulus of the amplitude.

|√3/2|²=3/4=0.75.

9Which statement correctly combines the spectral theorem and Stone's theorem for a self-adjoint A?

Unbounded operators require a natural square-integrability domain and a domain-qualified derivative.

The PVM formula defines the natural domain through the second spectral moment. Stone gives a strongly continuous unitary group, differentiable on D(A).

10For Deₙ=eₙ/n, zero is in continuous spectrum. Which spectral-measure statement is correct?

The range classification of D−0I and the atomicity of D's PVM answer different questions.

D is injective with dense non-surjective range at zero, yet D=Σ(1/n)|eₙ⟩⟨eₙ|. Continuous spectrum is not synonymous with a non-atomic spectral measure.
