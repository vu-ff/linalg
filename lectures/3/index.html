We have the objects of linear algebra—vector spaces—and their coordinate systems—bases. Now we study the maps between vector spaces that respect this structure. A linear map is a function that commutes with addition and scalar multiplication; equivalently, it preserves linear combinations, so it preserves superposition.

The quantum language in the next sentence is a forward preview, not an assumed prerequisite; inner products and these operator classes are defined in Lectures 7–10 and are not used in this lecture’s proofs. For now, read “self-adjoint” as the special kind of linear operator used to encode an observable, “unitary” as a linear transformation that preserves quantum lengths and overlaps, and “antiunitary” as its conjugate-linear symmetry analogue. A spectral projector selects the part of a state associated with one possible outcome; the later Born rule turns the squared length of that part into a probability, and the conditional update replaces the state by the normalized selected part. Thus the finite model uses self-adjoint operators for observables, unitary operators for closed-system evolution, unitary or antiunitary symmetries, and a conditional update that is not the observable’s linear action.

Two subspaces measure how a linear map behaves: its kernel (what it sends to zero) and its image (what it can produce). The central result of the lecture, the rank–nullity theorem, ties their dimensions together in a single equation dim⁡V=dim⁡null⁡T+dim⁡range⁡T\dim V=\dim\nul T+\dim\rng TdimV=dimnullT+dimrangeT, and from it we will read off when linear systems are solvable and when operators are invertible.

A linear map is determined by a basis

A linear map cannot be chosen freely at every point: (3.1) ties its values together. In fact its values on a single basis fix it completely, and those values may be assigned arbitrarily.

The algebra of linear maps

Linear maps can be added, scaled, and composed.

Kernel and injectivity

The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps

The kernel records what TTT destroys; the range records what it builds. The next theorem—the central result of the lecture—says their dimensions exactly account for the dimension of the domain (Figure 1).

Theorem 3.24 (Rank–nullity). Suppose VVV is finite-dimensional and T∈L(V,W)T\in\Lin(V,W)T∈L(V,W). Then range⁡T\rng TrangeT is finite-dimensional and dim⁡V=dim⁡null⁡T+dim⁡range⁡T.\dim V=\dim\nul T+\dim\rng T .dimV=dimnullT+dimrangeT.

Proof. Let u1,…,umu_1,\dots,u_mu1​,…,um​ be a basis of null⁡T\nul TnullT, so dim⁡null⁡T=m\dim\nul T=mdimnullT=m. Remark 3.34 (Forward preview: operators in quantum mechanics). Remark 3.38 (Physics: gauge fields and cohomology [self-study]). Quotient spaces appear at the foundations of modern physics.

Gauge symmetry. In electromagnetism the 444-potential AμA_\muAμ​ is not unique: two potentials related by Aμ→Aμ+∂μχA_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\chiAμ​→Aμ​+∂μ​χ produce the same field strength Fμν=∂μAν−∂νAμF_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\muFμν​=∂μ​Aν​−∂ν​Aμ​. The “pure gauge” configurations (those of the form ∂μχ\partial_\mu\chi∂μ​χ) form a subspace of the space of all potentials, and the space of physically distinct fields is the quotient. A gauge-fixed description chooses one representative from each coset—just as choosing xpx_pxp​ picks one solution from each coset xp+ker⁡Tx_p+\ker Txp​+kerT.

De Rham cohomology. For differential forms, the exterior derivative ddd satisfies d2=0d^2=0d2=0 (the analogue of a nilpotent, Lecture 6). The closed forms Zk=ker⁡dkZ^k=\ker d^kZk=kerdk and the exact forms Bk=range⁡dk−1B^k=\rng d^{k-1}Bk=rangedk−1 satisfy Bk⊆ZkB^k\subseteq Z^kBk⊆Zk, so the de Rham cohomology Hk=Zk/BkH^k=Z^k/B^kHk=Zk/Bk is a quotient of vector spaces. Its dimension records the topology of the manifold and governs the Aharonov–Bohm effect, magnetic monopoles, and Chern numbers.

BRST cohomology. In an anomaly-free BRST construction, physical states are identified with the appropriate ghost-number sector of the cohomology ker⁡Q/range⁡Q\ker Q/\rng QkerQ/rangeQ, where QQQ is the nilpotent BRST charge (Q2=0Q^2=0Q2=0); states that differ by QQQ-exact terms represent the same class. Nilpotency alone only makes this quotient well-defined. Positivity of the physical inner product, decoupling of unphysical degrees of freedom, and unitary physical dynamics require the additional standard consistency, domain, and completeness hypotheses of the particular gauge theory.

A linear map preserves linear combinations, hence superposition. It is fixed by its values on a basis, which may be chosen freely (Theorem 3.5). Its kernel null⁡T\nul TnullT and image range⁡T\rng TrangeT are subspaces; TTT is injective exactly when null⁡T={0}\nul T=\set0nullT={0} and surjective exactly when range⁡T=W\rng T=WrangeT=W. The rank–nullity theorem dim⁡V=dim⁡null⁡T+dim⁡range⁡T\dim V=\dim\nul T+\dim\rng TdimV=dimnullT+dimrangeT ties the two together, yielding at a glance that maps to smaller spaces cannot be injective, maps to larger spaces cannot be surjective, and operators on a finite-dimensional space are injective if and only if surjective. In coordinates this is the bookkeeping of pivots and free variables behind every linear system.

Self-check

10 questions on the ideas this lecture is most often misread on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

1T: R⁵ → R³ has rank 2. What is the dimension of its kernel?

dim V = dim ker T + rank T.

5 = dim ker T + 2, so dim ker T = 3.

2When is a linear map T injective?

Two vectors have the same image exactly when their difference lies in the kernel.

T is injective if and only if its kernel contains only the zero vector.

3What is the rank of the matrix [[1,2],[2,4]]?

The second column is a multiple of the first.

Both columns span the same line, so the rank is 1.

4Which map from R² to R² is not linear?

Every linear map must send the zero vector to the zero vector.

The translation sends (0,0) to (1,0), so it cannot be linear.

5T(e₁)=(1,2) and T(e₂)=(−1,1). What is the first coordinate of T(3,−2)?

Use T(3e₁−2e₂)=3T(e₁)−2T(e₂).

T(3,−2)=3(1,2)−2(−1,1)=(5,4), so the first coordinate is 5.

6Which statement holds for every linear map T: R⁵ → R³?

Compare the largest possible rank with the dimension of the domain.

Rank is at most 3, so nullity is at least 5−3=2. The kernel must contain non-zero vectors.

7For a matrix map x ↦ Ax, what spans the image?

Write Ax as a linear combination using the coordinates of x.

Ax is a linear combination of the columns of A, and every such combination occurs for some x.

8T: V → V is linear and V is finite-dimensional. If ker T={0}, what follows?

Use rank–nullity and compare the image dimension with dim V.

A zero-dimensional kernel gives rank T=dim V, so the image is all of V.

9For D: P₄(R) → P₃(R), Dp=p′, what is rank D?

Every polynomial of degree at most 3 has an antiderivative of degree at most 4.

D is surjective onto P₃(R), whose dimension is 4, so rank D=4.

10If Ax=b has one solution xₚ, what is its complete solution set?

Subtract xₚ from any other solution and apply A.

Every other solution differs from xₚ by a vector in ker A, and adding any kernel vector preserves Ax=b.
