Lecture 4 ended with a tantalising example: the right basis turned a reflection into the diagonal matrix diag⁡(1,−1)\diag(1,-1)diag(1,−1). The diagonal entries were the factors by which the operator stretched two special directions. This lecture names those objects—eigenvectors, the directions an operator merely scales, and eigenvalues, the scaling factors—and develops the tools to find them: the characteristic polynomial, the guaranteed existence of eigenvalues over C\CC, and upper-triangular form, in which the eigenvalues sit on the diagonal.

Unless an example is explicitly labelled otherwise, VVV is a nonzero finite-dimensional vector space over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C} and T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V). Thus the eigenvalue set studied here is the point spectrum; in this finite setting it agrees with the general spectrum defined by failure of invertibility. Lecture 14 separates point, continuous, and residual spectrum for operators on Hilbert space.

In the finite/discrete quantum model, a self-adjoint observable has eigenvalues for projective outcomes and eigenspaces for definite values. The equation H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ describes a discrete energy eigenstate. General observables may also have continuous spectrum, and atomic spectral lines record transition energy differences; Lectures 10 and 14 supply those qualifications.

Invariant subspaces and eigenvectors

Guiding question.

Which parts of a vector space keep their identity when an operator acts? Given T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V), we look first for subspaces that TTT preserves.

Definition 5.1 (Invariant subspace). A subspace U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V is invariant under TTT when every vector that starts in UUU remains in UUU after applying TTT: u∈U⟹Tu∈U.u\in U \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Tu\in U.u∈U⟹Tu∈U. Equivalently, T(U)⊆U.T(U)\subseteq U.T(U)⊆U.

Start with familiar invariant subspaces.

The following are always invariant: V,{0},null⁡T,range⁡T.V,\qquad \set0,\qquad \nul T,\qquad \rng T.V,{0},nullT,rangeT. The smallest nontrivial invariant subspaces are lines. For v≠0v\neq0v=0, the line span⁡(v)\spn(v)span(v) is invariant exactly when TvTvTv is a scalar multiple of vvv.

Definition 5.2 (Eigenvalue, eigenvector, eigenspace). A nonzero vector keeps its line under TTT precisely when Tv=λv,v≠0.Tv=\lambda v,\qquad v\neq0.Tv=λv,v=0.

  • Eigenvalue. The scalar λ∈F\lambda\in\Fλ∈F is the scale factor.

  • Eigenvector. The nonzero vector vvv is a direction that TTT preserves.

  • Eigenspace. All vectors with the same scale factor form the subspace E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv},E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I)=\set{v\in V : Tv=\lambda v},E(λ,T)=null(T−λI)={v∈V:Tv=λv}, including the zero vector.

The set of eigenvalues is the point spectrum. In our standing finite-dimensional setting it equals spec⁡(T)\spec(T)spec(T), defined by failure of invertibility. Lecture 14 explains why this equality can fail in infinite dimension.

Three equivalent tests.

The eigenspace is a kernel, so for a scalar λ\lambdaλ we may move freely along the chain λ∈spec⁡(T)  ⟺  E(λ,T)≠{0}  ⟺  T−λI is not injective  ⟺  T−λI is not invertible. \begin{aligned} \lambda\in\spec(T) &\iff E(\lambda,T)\neq\set0 \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not injective} \\ &\iff T-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible.} \end{aligned} λ∈spec(T)​⟺E(λ,T)={0}⟺T−λI is not injective⟺T−λI is not invertible.​ The last step uses the finite-dimensional equivalence “injective iff invertible” from Lecture 3. Geometrically, an eigenvector is a direction that TTT merely rescales (Figure 1).

Computing eigenvalues and eigenspaces

Guiding question.

How do we turn the geometric search for preserved directions into a reliable calculation? In coordinates, the link is singularity, detected by a determinant.

Definition 5.7 (Characteristic polynomial). For A∈Mn(F)A\in M_n(\F)A∈Mn​(F) (or for T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) via any basis), the characteristic polynomial is χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A). It is monic of degree nnn. Its roots are exactly the eigenvalues of AAA: χA(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.\chi_A(\lambda)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }A.χA​(λ)=0⟺λ is an eigenvalue of A.

The eigenpair method.

For a concrete matrix AAA, separate the scale-factor calculation from the direction calculation.

  1. Form the characteristic polynomial. χA(λ)=det⁡(λI−A).\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A).χA​(λ)=det(λI−A).

  2. Find the possible scale factors. χA(λ)=0.\chi_A(\lambda)=0.χA​(λ)=0.

  3. Find the directions for each root. E(λ,A)=ker⁡(A−λI).E(\lambda,A)=\ker(A-\lambda I).E(λ,A)=ker(A−λI).

  4. Check the complete result. Compare the eigenvalues with tr⁡A\tr AtrA and det⁡A\det AdetA, and count the independent eigenvectors.

Do not stop at the roots.

The polynomial finds possible scale factors; the kernel calculation finds the directions. Reporting roots without eigenspaces gives only half of the eigenpair calculation.

Remark 5.8 (Determinant facts we use). This course uses four determinant facts without reproving them here.

  • Invertibility. A square matrix is invertible iff det⁡A≠0\det A\neq0detA=0.

  • Products. det⁡(AB)=det⁡A det⁡B\det(AB)=\det A\,\det Bdet(AB)=detAdetB. In particular, det⁡(P−1AP)=det⁡A.\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det A.det(P−1AP)=detA.

  • Triangular form. The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.

  • Coefficients. det⁡(λI−A)=λn−(tr⁡A)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndet⁡A.\det(\lambda I-A) =\lambda^n-(\tr A)\lambda^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A.det(λI−A)=λn−(trA)λn−1+⋯+(−1)ndetA.

Each follows from cofactor expansion or row reduction and is proved in the course texts.

Why the roots are eigenvalues.

The key implications form one chain: χA(λ)=0  ⟺  λI−A is singular  ⟺  ker⁡(λI−A)≠{0}  ⟺  λ is an eigenvalue.\chi_A(\lambda)=0 \iff \lambda I-A\text{ is singular} \iff \ker(\lambda I-A)\neq\set0 \iff \lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue.}χA​(λ)=0⟺λI−A is singular⟺ker(λI−A)={0}⟺λ is an eigenvalue.

Why the polynomial belongs to the operator.

If two matrices represent the same operator in different bases, they are similar. Then det⁡(λI−P−1AP)=det⁡ ⁣(P−1(λI−A)P)=det⁡(λI−A),\det(\lambda I-P^{-1}AP)=\det\!\bigl(P^{-1}(\lambda I-A)P\bigr)=\det(\lambda I-A),det(λI−P−1AP)=det(P−1(λI−A)P)=det(λI−A), and therefore χP−1AP=χA.\chi_{P^{-1}AP}=\chi_A.χP−1AP​=χA​. Thus the characteristic polynomial and its eigenvalues do not depend on the chosen basis.

Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent

Guiding question.

When can several eigenvectors be used as independent coordinate directions? Distinct scale factors provide exactly the separation we need.

Theorem 5.15 (Independence of eigenvectors). Assume Tvj=λjvj(j=1,…,m),Tv_j=\lambda_jv_j\qquad (j=1,\dots,m),Tvj​=λj​vj​(j=1,…,m), where every vjv_jvj​ is nonzero and the eigenvalues λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​ are distinct. Why this matters.

Different eigenvalues automatically produce different independent directions. No row reduction of the eigenvectors is required.

Corollary 5.16 (At most dim⁡V\dim VdimV eigenvalues). An operator on an nnn-dimensional space has at most nnn distinct eigenvalues.

Proof. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues form an independent list, which can have at most n=dim⁡Vn=\dim Vn=dimV entries.

Existence of eigenvalues over \C

Guiding question.

Why can a real rotation have no eigenvector while a complex operator is guaranteed to have one? The difference is whether the characteristic polynomial must have a root.

Theorem 5.19 (Eigenvalues exist over C\CC). If V≠{0},dim⁡V<∞,V is a complex vector space,V\neq\set0,\qquad \dim V<\infty,\qquad V\text{ is a complex vector space},V={0},dimV<∞,V is a complex vector space, then every T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) has an eigenvalue.

Remark 5.20.

  • Where C\CC enters. The fundamental theorem of algebra supplies a root. A real polynomial need not have one.

  • What the theorem does not say. Complex scalars also encode phase and interference, but measurement values require self-adjointness and the Born postulate; eigenvalue existence alone is not a measurement theory.

Triangularisation is not diagonalisation.

Repeating the invariant-subspace argument gives commuting complex operators⟹simultaneous upper-triangularisation.\text{commuting complex operators} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{simultaneous upper-triangularisation}.commuting complex operators⟹simultaneous upper-triangularisation. For simultaneous diagonalisation, the operators must additionally be diagonalizable. Pairwise commuting normal operators satisfy this later criterion. The identity and a non-diagonalizable Jordan block commute, but they cannot be simultaneously diagonalized.

Multiplicity and defective operators

Guiding question.

A repeated eigenvalue appears several times in the characteristic polynomial. Does it necessarily provide the same number of independent eigenvectors?

Two multiplicities.

For an eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, keep two counts separate:

  • Algebraic multiplicity: the multiplicity of λ\lambdaλ as a root of χT\chi_TχT​.

  • Geometric multiplicity: dim⁡E(λ,T)=dim⁡ker⁡(T−λI).\dim E(\lambda,T)=\dim\ker(T-\lambda I).dimE(λ,T)=dimker(T−λI).

The calculation in Example 5.13 shows that these counts can differ.

Proposition 5.24 (Geometric ≤\le≤ algebraic). For every eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, dim⁡E(λ,T)≤mult⁡χT(λ).\dim E(\lambda,T) \le \operatorname{mult}_{\chi_T}(\lambda).dimE(λ,T)≤multχT​​(λ).

Proof. Let g=dim⁡E(λ,T)g=\dim E(\lambda,T)g=dimE(λ,T).

  1. Choose a basis v1,…,vgv_1,\dots,v_gv1​,…,vg​ of E(λ,T)E(\lambda,T)E(λ,T) and extend it to a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ of VVV.

  2. Since Tvk=λvkTv_k=\lambda v_kTvk​=λvk​ for k≤gk\le gk≤g, the matrix has block form M(T)=(λIgB0C).\Mat(T)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda I_g & B\\[2pt]0&C\end{pmatrix}.M(T)=(λIg​0​BC​).

  3. Its characteristic polynomial therefore factors as χT(t)=(t−λ)gdet⁡ ⁣(tIn−g−C).\chi_T(t) =(t-\lambda)^g\det\!\bigl(tI_{n-g}-C\bigr).χT​(t)=(t−λ)gdet(tIn−g​−C).

  4. Hence (t−λ)g(t-\lambda)^g(t−λ)g divides χT(t)\chi_T(t)χT​(t). The root multiplicity of λ\lambdaλ is at least ggg.

Upper-triangular matrices

Guiding question.

What useful structure survives when an operator has too few eigenvectors for a diagonal basis? Over C\CC, we can still find a basis that makes its matrix upper triangular.

The invariant-flag picture.

An ordered basis produces nested subspaces span⁡(v1)⊆span⁡(v1,v2)⊆⋯⊆span⁡(v1,…,vn)=V.\spn(v_1) \subseteq\spn(v_1,v_2) \subseteq\cdots\subseteq \spn(v_1,\dots,v_n)=V.span(v1​)⊆span(v1​,v2​)⊆⋯⊆span(v1​,…,vn​)=V. Upper-triangular form means that TTT preserves every subspace in this flag.

Core versus extension.

The triangularizability statement below and the invariant-flag criterion are core. The quotient-induction construction in its proof is an extension sketch.

The payoff.

For a triangular matrix, the spectrum can be read directly from its diagonal.

Theorem 5.32 (Eigenvalues sit on the diagonal). Physics bridge: finite and discrete observables

The remaining examples interpret the algebra in finite quantum models. They add physical meaning, not new core techniques.

Example 5.35 (The Pauli matrix σx\sigma_xσx​). In units of ℏ/2\hbar/2ℏ/2, consider σx=(0110).\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.σx​=(01​10​). Physical meaning.

In the finite ideal-projective model, ±1\pm1±1 are the possible outcomes and the two eigenspaces are the corresponding definite-value states. The Born rule supplies probabilities; Lecture 14 replaces this finite list by a spectral measure in the general setting.

Example 5.36 (Compatible and incompatible observables). Compare σx=(0110),σz=(100−1).\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.σx​=(01​10​),σz​=(10​0−1​). Interpretation.

Commuting diagonalizable operators share an eigenbasis. These two self-adjoint operators do not commute and share no eigenbasis, anticipating the uncertainty relation of Lecture 9.

Remark 5.37 (Finite/discrete observables and energy levels).

  • Finite or discrete pure-point model. H^ψ=Eψ\hat H\psi=E\psiH^ψ=Eψ identifies energy eigenvalues and stationary state vectors. The later self-adjoint spectral theorem makes distinct eigenspaces orthogonal.

  • General self-adjoint observable. Continuous spectrum may be present and normalizable eigenvectors may be absent. Outcomes and probabilities then require a projection-valued measure and the Born rule (Lecture 14), not the fundamental theorem of algebra.

Remark 5.38. Atomic spectral lines record allowed energy differences: hν=Eupper−Elower.h\nu=E_{\mathrm{upper}}-E_{\mathrm{lower}}.hν=Eupper​−Elower​. An eigenpair separates scale from direction: Tv=λv,E(λ,T)=null⁡(T−λI).Tv=\lambda v, \qquad E(\lambda,T)=\nul(T-\lambda I).Tv=λv,E(λ,T)=null(T−λI). In finite dimension, λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue exactly when T−λIT-\lambda IT−λI is not invertible.

The characteristic polynomial finds the scale factors; kernel calculations find their directions. Distinct eigenvalues give independent eigenvectors, and nnn distinct eigenvalues on an nnn-dimensional space force an eigenbasis.

Over C\CC, the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees at least one eigenvalue. Repeating that existence argument along invariant subspaces yields upper-triangular form, where every eigenvalue is visible on the diagonal.
