Lecture 5 found the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of an operator and showed that, with enough independent eigenvectors, its matrix becomes diagonal. This lecture turns that observation into a working theory. We characterize exactly when an operator is diagonalizable, prove the Cayley–Hamilton theorem (every operator satisfies its own characteristic equation), and use diagonalization to compute functions of operators—above all the exponential etAe^{tA}etA, which solves linear differential equations and, in the form e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ, generates time evolution in quantum mechanics.

Throughout this lecture VVV is finite-dimensional over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C} and every matrix represents an endomorphism of such a space, unless an extension is explicitly labelled otherwise. Characteristic polynomials and matrix power series below belong to this finite setting; Lecture 14 develops bounded and unbounded Hilbert-space functional calculus.

The unifying idea is simple: in an eigenbasis an operator is just a list of independent scalings, so anything we can do to numbers—raise to a power, take an exponential, apply a function—we can do to the operator one eigenvalue at a time.

Any ket used in the quantum previews below is only Dirac notation for a vector: ∣v⟩\ket v∣v⟩ means vvv, and the matrix columns are the images of ordered basis vectors. Bras and the inner-product rules are introduced in Lecture 7.

Minimal-polynomial criteria are retained only for extension recognition; they are not a core use, practice, or progress requirement.

Diagonalizable operators

Definition 6.1 (Diagonalizable). An operator T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) is diagonalizable if VVV has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of TTT; equivalently, if M(T)\Mat(T)M(T) is a diagonal matrix in some basis.

The two formulations agree because the matrix of TTT in a basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ is diagonal exactly when Tvk=λkvkTv_k=\lambda_kv_kTvk​=λk​vk​ for each kkk, i.e. when every basis vector is an eigenvector. The following theorem collects the practical tests.

Theorem 6.2 (Conditions for diagonalizability). Let T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) with dim⁡V=n\dim V=ndimV=n and distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​. The following are equivalent.

  1. TTT is diagonalizable.

  2. VVV has a basis of eigenvectors of TTT.

  3. V=E(λ1,T)⊕⋯⊕E(λm,T)V=E(\lambda_1,T)\dsum\cdots\dsum E(\lambda_m,T)V=E(λ1​,T)⊕⋯⊕E(λm​,T).

  4. dim⁡V=dim⁡E(λ1,T)+⋯+dim⁡E(λm,T)\dim V=\dim E(\lambda_1,T)+\cdots+\dim E(\lambda_m,T)dimV=dimE(λ1​,T)+⋯+dimE(λm​,T).

Proof. (1)⇔\Leftrightarrow⇔(2) is the definition. For (2)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(4): a basis of eigenvectors splits into vectors lying in the various eigenspaces, so ∑kdim⁡E(λk,T)≥n\sum_k\dim E(\lambda_k,T)\ge n∑k​dimE(λk​,T)≥n; the reverse inequality always holds because the eigenspaces form a direct sum (Lecture 5), giving equality. For (4)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(3): the sum E(λ1,T)+⋯+E(λm,T)E(\lambda_1,T)+\cdots+E(\lambda_m,T)E(λ1​,T)+⋯+E(λm​,T) is direct (Lecture 5), so its dimension is ∑kdim⁡E(λk,T)=n=dim⁡V\sum_k\dim E(\lambda_k,T)=n=\dim V∑k​dimE(λk​,T)=n=dimV; a subspace of full dimension is all of VVV. For (3)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(2): concatenating bases of the eigenspaces yields a basis of VVV consisting of eigenvectors. 

Corollary 6.3 (Distinct eigenvalues suffice). If T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) has dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues, then TTT is diagonalizable.

Proof. The corresponding eigenvectors are independent (Lecture 5), and an independent list of length dim⁡V\dim VdimV is a basis; condition (2) holds. 

Distinctness is sufficient but not necessary: the identity has the single eigenvalue 111 yet is already diagonal. What fails for a defective operator is condition (4)—some eigenspace is too small.

Remark 6.11 (Decoupling). Physically, passing to the eigenbasis is decoupling. Vibrating normal modes belong to the second-order mechanical system in the next example.

The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

A striking fact ties an operator to its characteristic polynomial χT(λ)=det⁡(λI−T)\chi_T(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-T)χT​(λ)=det(λI−T).

Theorem 6.13 (Cayley–Hamilton). Every operator TTT on a finite-dimensional real or complex vector space satisfies its own characteristic equation: χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0.

Proof for diagonalizable TTT. Write A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1 with D=diag⁡(λ1,…,λn)D=\diag(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​). For any polynomial ppp, p(A)=P p(D) P−1p(A)=P\,p(D)\,P^{-1}p(A)=Pp(D)P−1 and p(D)=diag⁡(p(λ1),…,p(λn))p(D)=\diag(p(\lambda_1),\dots,p(\lambda_n))p(D)=diag(p(λ1​),…,p(λn​)). Taking p=χAp=\chi_Ap=χA​ and using that each λk\lambda_kλk​ is a root of χA\chi_AχA​, χA(A)=P diag⁡(χA(λ1),…,χA(λn))P−1=P diag⁡(0,…,0) P−1=0.\chi_A(A)=P\,\diag\bigl(\chi_A(\lambda_1),\dots,\chi_A(\lambda_n)\bigr)P^{-1} =P\,\diag(0,\dots,0)\,P^{-1}=0 . χA​(A)=Pdiag(χA​(λ1​),…,χA​(λn​))P−1=Pdiag(0,…,0)P−1=0. 

Proof in general. Over C\CC every operator has an upper-triangular matrix (Lecture 5, where triangularizability is sketched) with diagonal entries λ1,…,λn\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_nλ1​,…,λn​, and then χT(λ)=(λ−λ1)⋯(λ−λn)\chi_T(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_1)\cdots(\lambda-\lambda_n)χT​(λ)=(λ−λ1​)⋯(λ−λn​). The triangular-product lemma of Lecture 5 gives (T−λ1I)⋯(T−λnI)=0(T-\lambda_1I)\cdots(T-\lambda_nI)=0(T−λ1​I)⋯(T−λn​I)=0 for such an operator, and that product is exactly χT(T)\chi_T(T)χT​(T), so χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0.

For a real operator, apply the proved complex result to its complexification. The matrix identity χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0 has real coefficients and the same real matrix entries, so it descends to the original real space.

Functions of operators

Remark 6.25 (Identities transport to operators). Any scalar identity among functions holds for diagonalizable operators, since both sides act eigenvalue by eigenvalue. Proposition 6.26 (Spectral decomposition). If TTT is diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues λ1,…,λm\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_mλ1​,…,λm​, there are projections P1,…,PmP_1,\dots,P_mP1​,…,Pm​ (each onto an eigenspace along the others) with PiPj=0  (i≠j),P1+⋯+Pm=I,T=λ1P1+⋯+λmPm,P_iP_j=0\ \ (i\neq j),\qquad P_1+\cdots+P_m=I,\qquad T=\lambda_1P_1+\cdots+\lambda_mP_m,Pi​Pj​=0  (i=j),P1​+⋯+Pm​=I,T=λ1​P1​+⋯+λm​Pm​, and consequently f(T)=∑kf(λk)Pkf(T)=\sum_k f(\lambda_k)P_kf(T)=∑k​f(λk​)Pk​ for any f:spec⁡(T)→Ff:\spec(T)\to\Ff:spec(T)→F.

Spectral mapping and time evolution

Functions act on the spectrum in the obvious way.

Proposition 6.28 (Spectral mapping). If TTT is diagonalizable over F\FF and f:spec⁡(T)→Ff:\spec(T)\to\Ff:spec(T)→F, then spec⁡(f(T))={f(λ):λ∈spec⁡(T)}\spec\bigl(f(T)\bigr)=\set{f(\lambda):\lambda\in\spec(T)}spec(f(T))={f(λ):λ∈spec(T)}, and every eigenvector of TTT for λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvector of f(T)f(T)f(T) for f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ).

Proof. If Tv=λvTv=\lambda vTv=λv then f(T)v=Pf(D)P−1vf(T)v=Pf(D)P^{-1}vf(T)v=Pf(D)P−1v acts on the eigenvector vvv by the scalar f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ), since in eigen-coordinates vvv is a standard basis vector and f(D)f(D)f(D) scales it by f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ). Conversely, the same eigenbasis diagonalizes f(T)f(T)f(T), and its diagonal entries are exactly the values f(λk)f(\lambda_k)f(λk​). Therefore no additional eigenvalues of f(T)f(T)f(T) occur. 

The converse inclusion for eigenvectors fails when fff is not injective on the spectrum: for T=diag⁡(1,−1)T=\diag(1,-1)T=diag(1,−1) and f(λ)=λ2f(\lambda)=\lambda^2f(λ)=λ2 we get f(T)=If(T)=If(T)=I, every nonzero vector of which is an eigenvector, while TTT has only two eigendirections. Distinct eigenvalues can be merged by fff, and merging enlarges an eigenspace.

Example 6.29 (Spectrum of a function). For A=(2112)A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)A=(21​12​) with spectrum {1,3}\set{1,3}{1,3}, the operator eAe^{A}eA has spectrum {e, e3}\set{e,\,e^{3}}{e,e3} and A10A^{10}A10 has spectrum {1, 310}\set{1,\,3^{10}}{1,310}, all with the same eigenvectors (1,−1)(1,-1)(1,−1) and (1,1)(1,1)(1,1). Example 6.30 (Oscillation from imaginary eigenvalues). The generator A=(0−ωω0)A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-\omega\\\omega&0\end{smallmatrix} \right)A=(0ω​−ω0​) has eigenvalues ±iω\pm i\omega±iω, and a short computation (or the cos⁡/sin⁡\cos/\sincos/sin split, since A2=−ω2IA^2=-\omega^2 IA2=−ω2I) gives etA=(cos⁡ωt−sin⁡ωtsin⁡ωtcos⁡ωt).e^{tA}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t&-\sin\omega t\\ \sin\omega t&\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}.etA=(cosωtsinωt​−sinωtcosωt​). Remark 6.31 (Quantum time evolution). In the finite-dimensional, time-independent, closed-system model, a state vector evolves by ∣ψ(t)⟩=e−iH^t/ℏ∣ψ(0)⟩\ket{\psi(t)}=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{\psi(0)}∣ψ(t)⟩=e−iH^t/ℏ∣ψ(0)⟩. In an energy eigenbasis H^=diag⁡(E1,…,En)\hat H=\diag(E_1,\dots,E_n)H^=diag(E1​,…,En​), so e−iH^t/ℏ=diag⁡(e−iE1t/ℏ,…,e−iEnt/ℏ):e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}=\diag\bigl(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar},\dots,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\bigr):e−iH^t/ℏ=diag(e−iE1​t/ℏ,…,e−iEn​t/ℏ): each stationary state merely acquires a phase e−iEnt/ℏe^{-iE_nt/\hbar}e−iEn​t/ℏ, and a general state is a superposition of these independently rotating phases. Diagonalizing the Hamiltonian solves this finite pure-point dynamics. General spectral integrals and domain questions are deferred to Lecture 14.

Remark 6.32 (Finite/discrete simultaneous diagonalization). Commuting diagonalizable operators can be brought to diagonal form in a single basis—the common eigenvectors of Lecture 5. Physically, finite-dimensional commuting self-adjoint observables share an orthonormal eigenbasis, so a state can have simultaneously definite values for both. For general Hilbert-space observables the corresponding statement uses joint spectral measures and projection-valued measures rather than necessarily an ordinary eigenbasis; Lecture 14 gives that outlook.

Extension: when diagonalization fails

The entire section is optional extension material. Jordan form, Jordan–Chevalley decomposition, defective exponentials, exceptional points, and ladder/cohomology analogies do not count toward core practice or progress.

Not every operator is diagonalizable, but the generalized eigenvectors of Lecture 5 restore order. The obstruction is a nilpotent operator—one with Nk=0N^k=0Nk=0 for some kkk—the part of an operator that no change of basis can diagonalize away. On a single defective eigenvalue A=λI+NA=\lambda I+NA=λI+N with NNN nilpotent, and since λI\lambda IλI and NNN commute the nilpotent factor etNe^{tN}etN terminates: etA=eλt etN=eλt(I+tN+t22!N2+⋯+tk−1(k−1)!Nk−1).e^{tA}=e^{\lambda t}\,e^{tN} =e^{\lambda t}\Bigl(I+tN+\tfrac{t^2}{2!}N^2+\cdots+\tfrac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}N^{k-1}\Bigr).etA=eλtetN=eλt(I+tN+2!t2​N2+⋯+(k−1)!tk−1​Nk−1). For the defective shear block A=(λ10λ)A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda \end{smallmatrix}\right)A=(λ0​1λ​), the nilpotent part N=(0100)N=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0 \end{smallmatrix}\right)N=(00​10​) satisfies N2=0N^2=0N2=0, so etA=eλt(1t01).e^{tA}=e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.etA=eλt(10​t1​). The extra factor ttt is the signature of a defective eigenvalue: it produces solutions t eλtt\,e^{\lambda t}teλt, the same ones that appear for repeated roots of a linear differential equation.

Theorem 6.33 (Jordan normal form). Every operator on a finite-dimensional complex space has a basis of generalized eigenvectors in which its matrix is block diagonal, each block a Jordan block J=λI+N,N=(010⋱⋱10),J=\lambda I+N,\qquad N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&\\&0&\ddots&\\&&\ddots&1\\&&&0\end{pmatrix},J=λI+N,N=​0​10​⋱⋱​10​​, carrying the eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ on the diagonal and the nilpotent shift NNN on the superdiagonal. Each block is one Jordan chain of generalized eigenvectors (Lecture 5).

The shift NNN sends each generalized eigenvector to the one below it, ej↦ej−1↦⋯↦e1↦0e_j\mapsto e_{j-1}\mapsto\cdots\mapsto e_1\mapsto0ej​↦ej−1​↦⋯↦e1​↦0—a ladder that descends to zero in finitely many steps, which is exactly what nilpotency means.

Theorem 6.34 (Jordan–Chevalley decomposition). On a complex vector space of finite dimension, every operator AAA splits uniquely as A=D+N,DN=ND,A=D+N,\qquad DN=ND,A=D+N,DN=ND, with DDD diagonalizable and NNN nilpotent, and both DDD and NNN polynomials in AAA.

Because DDD and NNN are polynomials in AAA, they commute with everything AAA commutes with, so the splitting respects any symmetry AAA has—this is what makes it more than a normal-form trick. It is also the cleanest route to etA=etDetNe^{tA}=e^{tD}e^{tN}etA=etDetN for an arbitrary matrix: the diagonal factor supplies the oscillation and decay, the nilpotent factor the polynomial-in-ttt corrections.

Remark 6.35 (Nilpotents and ladder operators). Nilpotent operators are the ladder operators of physics, seen in finite dimensions. On a spin-jjj representation (dimension 2j+12j+12j+1) the raising operator J+J_+J+​ steps ∣m⟩↦∣m+1⟩\ket m\mapsto\ket{m+1}∣m⟩↦∣m+1⟩ until it annihilates the top state, so J+ 2j+1=0J_+^{\,2j+1}=0J+2j+1​=0: J+J_+J+​ is nilpotent, and its action is precisely the shift NNN of a Jordan block. The harmonic-oscillator lowering operator truncated to NNN levels is nilpotent in the same way (aN=0a^N=0aN=0), and the oscillator ladder of Lectures 13–14 is its infinite-dimensional limit. Nilpotency squared to zero, Q2=0Q^2=0Q2=0, is also the defining relation of the supercharge in supersymmetric quantum mechanics, of the BRST charge, and of the exterior derivative—the algebra behind cohomology.

Remark 6.36 (Where defective operators are physical, and a note on self-study). In finite dimension, self-adjointness rules defectiveness out entirely (Lectures 9–10), so finite observables are diagonalizable. General self-adjoint operators instead use spectral measures and need not have an ordinary eigenbasis. Defective operators belong instead to non-Hermitian physics: the critically damped oscillator, whose repeated root gives the tell-tale t e−γtt\,e^{-\gamma t}te−γt above, and the exceptional points of PTPTPT-symmetric and open systems, where two eigenvectors coalesce and the Hamiltonian becomes a genuine Jordan block—a measurable effect in optics and cavity physics. For a self-adjoint family H0+εVH_0+\varepsilon VH0​+εV no such coalescence can occur at real ε\varepsilonε; it can at complex ε\varepsilonε, and how near those points come to the real axis is exactly what limits the perturbation series of optional Lecture 15. The Jordan and Jordan–Chevalley results are offered here for self-study: we state their structure without proving the construction, and carry forward only the principle—diagonal where possible, a nilpotent correction where not.

On a finite-dimensional real or complex space, an operator is diagonalizable iff it has a basis of eigenvectors, equivalently iff its eigenspace dimensions sum to dim⁡V\dim VdimV; having dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues is a convenient sufficient condition. In an eigenbasis A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1, so powers and functions are computed one eigenvalue at a time: Ak=PDkP−1A^k=PD^kP^{-1}Ak=PDkP−1 and f(T)=Pf(D)P−1f(T)=Pf(D)P^{-1}f(T)=Pf(D)P−1 for f:spec⁡(T)→Ff:\spec(T)\to\Ff:spec(T)→F, with the finite matrix exponential etAe^{tA}etA solving x˙=Ax\dot x=Axx˙=Ax. Every such operator satisfies its own characteristic equation χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0 (Cayley–Hamilton), which expresses inverses and high powers as low-degree polynomials in TTT. Spectral mapping sends eigenvalues λ\lambdaλ to f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ); in quantum mechanics this is exactly why diagonalizing the Hamiltonian solves the time evolution e−iH^t/ℏe^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}e−iH^t/ℏ.

  • Diagonalizable—has an eigenbasis   ⟺  \iff⟺ eigenspace dimensions sum to dim⁡V\dim VdimV; dim⁡V\dim VdimV distinct eigenvalues suffice.
  • Diagonalized form—A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1, so powers and functions act one eigenvalue at a time; etAe^{tA}etA solves x˙=Ax\dot x=Axx˙=Ax.
  • Cayley–Hamilton—χT(T)=0\chi_T(T)=0χT​(T)=0; expresses inverses and high powers as low-degree polynomials in TTT.
  • Spectral mapping—fff sends eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ to f(λ)f(\lambda)f(λ)—why diagonalizing HHH solves e−iHt/ℏe^{-iHt/\hbar}e−iHt/ℏ.
  • Jordan form—extension recognition only: the canonical form when diagonalization fails (self-study; not core progress).
