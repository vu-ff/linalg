Lecture 7 ended with a finite-dimensional question: can we find a basis of mutually orthogonal unit vectors, and expand every vector as a finite sum in it? Over R\RR or C\CC, this lecture answers yes. The Gram–Schmidt procedure manufactures an orthonormal basis from any basis, and in such a basis everything becomes simple: coordinates are just inner products, v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}\,e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​; the norm is the Pythagorean sum ∥v∥2=∑k∣⟨ek,v⟩∣2\norm v^2=\sum_k\abs{\inner{e_k}{v}}^2∥v∥2=∑k​∣⟨ek​,v⟩∣2; and the identity resolves as ∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. We then use orthonormality to solve the best-approximation problem (orthogonal projection finds the closest point of a subspace) and to prove the Riesz representation theorem, which says every bra is the partner of a unique ket. These are the daily tools of quantum mechanics: expanding a state in an energy eigenbasis, computing amplitudes, and projecting onto measurement outcomes.

Orthonormal bases

Definition 8.1 (Orthonormal list and basis). A list e1,…,eme_1,\dots,e_me1​,…,em​ is orthonormal if ⟨ej,ek⟩=δjk\inner{e_j}{e_k}=\delta_{jk}⟨ej​,ek​⟩=δjk​ (each is a unit vector, distinct ones orthogonal). An orthonormal list that is also a basis is an orthonormal basis (ONB).

Lemma 8.2 (Orthonormal lists are independent). Every orthonormal list is linearly independent.

Proof. If ∑kckek=0\sum_k c_ke_k=0∑k​ck​ek​=0, pairing with eje_jej​ gives 0=⟨ej,∑kckek⟩=∑kckδjk=cj0=\inner{e_j}{\sum_k c_ke_k}=\sum_k c_k\delta_{jk}=c_j0=⟨ej​,∑k​ck​ek​⟩=∑k​ck​δjk​=cj​. So every coefficient vanishes. 

The point of an ONB is that coordinates require no linear system—they are inner products.

The second identity is Parseval’s relation.

Proof. Write v=∑kckekv=\sum_k c_ke_kv=∑k​ck​ek​ (possible since the eke_kek​ are a basis). Pairing with eje_jej​ and using orthonormality, ⟨ej,v⟩=∑kckδjk=cj\inner{e_j}{v}=\sum_k c_k\delta_{jk}=c_j⟨ej​,v⟩=∑k​ck​δjk​=cj​, which gives the first formula. Corollary 8.4 (Inner products from coordinates). In an orthonormal basis, ⟨u,v⟩=∑k⟨ek,u⟩‾ ⟨ek,v⟩\inner{u}{v}=\sum_k\conj{\inner{e_k}{u}}\,\inner{e_k}{v}⟨u,v⟩=∑k​⟨ek​,u⟩​⟨ek​,v⟩. Proof. Remark 8.5 (Completeness relation). In Dirac notation the expansion reads ∣v⟩=∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek,v⟩=(∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣)∣v⟩,\ket v=\sum_k\ket{e_k}\inner{e_k}{v} =\Bigl(\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}\Bigr)\ket v,∣v⟩=k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​,v⟩=(k∑​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣)∣v⟩, so an orthonormal basis satisfies the completeness relation ∑k∣ek⟩⟨ek∣=I\sum_k\ketbra{e_k}{e_k}=I∑k​∣ek​⟩⟨ek​∣=I. Inserting this resolution of the identity between symbols is the basic move of quantum-mechanical calculation.

The Gram–Schmidt procedure

Theorem 8.10 (Gram–Schmidt). Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis. Concretely, from any basis v1,…,vnv_1,\dots,v_nv1​,…,vn​ one builds an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ with span⁡(e1,…,ek)=span⁡(v1,…,vk)\spn(e_1,\dots,e_k)=\spn(v_1,\dots,v_k)span(e1​,…,ek​)=span(v1​,…,vk​) for every kkk, by e1=v1∥v1∥,ek=wk∥wk∥,wk=vk−∑j<k⟨ej,vk⟩ ej.e_1=\frac{v_1}{\norm{v_1}},\qquad e_k=\frac{w_k}{\norm{w_k}},\qquad w_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\inner{e_j}{v_k}\,e_j .e1​=∥v1​∥v1​​,ek​=∥wk​∥wk​​,wk​=vk​−j<k∑​⟨ej​,vk​⟩ej​.

Proof. We induct on kkk, assuming e1,…,ek−1e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}e1​,…,ek−1​ orthonormal with the right span. The numerator wk=vk−∑j<k⟨ej,vk⟩ejw_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\inner{e_j}{v_k}e_jwk​=vk​−∑j<k​⟨ej​,vk​⟩ej​ is nonzero, else vkv_kvk​ would lie in span⁡(v1,…,vk−1)\spn(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1})span(v1​,…,vk−1​), contradicting independence. For i<ki<ki<k, ⟨ei,wk⟩=⟨ei,vk⟩−∑j<k⟨ej,vk⟩⟨ei,ej⟩=⟨ei,vk⟩−⟨ei,vk⟩=0,\inner{e_i}{w_k}=\inner{e_i}{v_k}-\sum_{j<k}\inner{e_j}{v_k}\inner{e_i}{e_j} =\inner{e_i}{v_k}-\inner{e_i}{v_k}=0,⟨ei​,wk​⟩=⟨ei​,vk​⟩−j<k∑​⟨ej​,vk​⟩⟨ei​,ej​⟩=⟨ei​,vk​⟩−⟨ei​,vk​⟩=0, so ek=wk/∥wk∥e_k=w_k/\norm{w_k}ek​=wk​/∥wk​∥ is a unit vector orthogonal to the earlier ones. Since vkv_kvk​ and eke_kek​ differ only by a scalar and a combination of e1,…,ek−1e_1,\dots,e_{k-1}e1​,…,ek−1​, the spans agree. After nnn steps we have nnn orthonormal vectors, a basis by Lemma 8.2.

Remark 8.15 (Gram determinant and volume). In a real inner-product space, the determinant of the Gram matrix Gjk=⟨vj,vk⟩G_{jk}=\inner{v_j}{v_k}Gjk​=⟨vj​,vk​⟩ equals the squared volume of the parallelepiped spanned by v1,…,vmv_1,\dots,v_mv1​,…,vm​, and it is nonzero exactly when the vectors are independent (Lecture 7). Gram–Schmidt computes the same content step by step: the norms ∥wk∥\norm{w_k}∥wk​∥ are the successive “heights” whose product is that volume.

Corollary 8.17 (Extending to an orthonormal basis). Every orthonormal list in a finite-dimensional VVV extends to an orthonormal basis of VVV.

Proof. Extend the orthonormal list to a basis (Lecture 2), then run Gram–Schmidt; the already-orthonormal vectors are unchanged, and the rest become an orthonormal completion. 

Remark 8.18 (Bridge: the QRQRQR factorization). Collecting the relations vk=∑j≤krjkejv_k=\sum_{j\le k}r_{jk}e_jvk​=∑j≤k​rjk​ej​ into matrices says A=QRA=QRA=QR: any matrix with independent columns factors as a matrix QQQ with orthonormal columns times an upper-triangular RRR. For dense numerical least squares, Householder QR is a standard robust choice; Gram–Schmidt remains the transparent geometric construction.

Remark 8.19 (Orthonormal bases are far from unique). For a fixed ordered independent input, the displayed normalization ek=wk/∥wk∥e_k=w_k/\norm{w_k}ek​=wk​/∥wk​∥ gives a unique ordered output. Changing the input order changes that output; deliberately changing signs or complex phases afterwards produces other ONBs. Every nonzero complex space, and every real space of dimension at least two, has infinitely many ONBs; R\RR has only (1)(1)(1) and (−1)(-1)(−1), while the zero space has one. Any two ONBs are related by a unitary change of basis—the inner-product-preserving operators of Lecture 9.

Remark 8.20 (Bridge: concise numerical caution). In floating-point arithmetic the classical formula can lose orthogonality through accumulated rounding. Modified Gram–Schmidt, which subtracts each projection as soon as it is computed, is usually more stable than the classical form; Householder QR is a standard robust choice for dense least-squares work. The mathematics is identical, the numerical behaviour is not.

Orthogonal projection and best approximation

The Riesz representation theorem

A linear functional on VVV is a linear map φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F, where F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C}. In an inner product space every functional is “take the inner product with a fixed vector.”

Theorem 8.34 (Riesz representation). Let VVV be a finite-dimensional inner-product space over F\FF. For every linear functional φ ⁣:V→F\varphi\colon V\to\Fφ:V→F there is a unique w∈Vw\in Vw∈V with φ(v)=⟨w,v⟩for all v∈V.\varphi(v)=\inner{w}{v}\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.φ(v)=⟨w,v⟩for all v∈V.

Proof. Fix an orthonormal basis e1,…,ene_1,\dots,e_ne1​,…,en​ and set w=∑kφ(ek)‾ ekw=\sum_k\conj{\varphi(e_k)}\,e_kw=∑k​φ(ek​)​ek​. Then for any v=∑k⟨ek,v⟩ekv=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_kv=∑k​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​, ⟨w,v⟩=∑kφ(ek)⟨ek,v⟩=φ(∑k⟨ek,v⟩ek)=φ(v),\inner{w}{v}=\sum_k\varphi(e_k)\inner{e_k}{v} =\varphi\Bigl(\sum_k\inner{e_k}{v}e_k\Bigr)=\varphi(v),⟨w,v⟩=k∑​φ(ek​)⟨ek​,v⟩=φ(k∑​⟨ek​,v⟩ek​)=φ(v), using ⟨w,ek⟩=φ(ek)\inner{w}{e_k}=\varphi(e_k)⟨w,ek​⟩=φ(ek​) in the first step and linearity of φ\varphiφ in the second. For uniqueness, if ⟨w,v⟩=⟨w′,v⟩\inner{w}{v}=\inner{w'}{v}⟨w,v⟩=⟨w′,v⟩ for all vvv then ⟨w−w′,v⟩=0\inner{w-w'}{v}=0⟨w−w′,v⟩=0 for all vvv; taking v=w−w′v=w-w'v=w−w′ forces w=w′w=w'w=w′. 

Remark 8.35 (Bra–ket duality). Riesz says exactly that the map ∣w⟩↦⟨w∣\ket w\mapsto\bra w∣w⟩↦⟨w∣ is a bijection: every bra (linear functional φ\varphiφ) is ⟨w∣\bra w⟨w∣ for a unique ket ∣w⟩\ket w∣w⟩. The bra space is thus an exact (conjugate-linear) copy of the ket space—the rigorous content of Dirac’s notation. The conjugation w=∑kφ(ek)‾ekw=\sum_k\conj{\varphi(e_k)}e_kw=∑k​φ(ek​)​ek​ is the same conjugation that makes ∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣\ket\phi\mapsto\bra\phi∣ϕ⟩↦⟨ϕ∣ conjugate-linear over C\CC. Over R\RR this correspondence is linear because conjugation is trivial.

Physics bridge: measurement, expansion, and what comes next

In the finite/discrete ideal-projective model, scalar outcomes are eigenvalues λ\lambdaλ, including degenerate ones. Their spectral projections PλP_\lambdaPλ​ have mutually orthogonal eigenspaces, and a normalized pure-state representative ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩ yields outcome λ\lambdaλ with probability ∥Pλψ∥2\norm{P_\lambda\psi}^2∥Pλ​ψ∥2. Parseval expresses normalization as the sum of these probabilities. The Lueders update postulate sends a state with nonzero outcome probability to the normalized projection Pλ∣ψ⟩/∥Pλψ∥P_\lambda\ket\psi/\norm{P_\lambda\psi}Pλ​∣ψ⟩/∥Pλ​ψ∥. General measurements lie outside this core model; continuous-spectrum spectral measures wait until Lecture 14.

Example 8.40 (Physics bridge: probabilities sum to one). If ∣ψ⟩=13∣0⟩+23 ∣1⟩\ket\psi=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\ket0+\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,\ket1∣ψ⟩=3​1​∣0⟩+32​​∣1⟩, the outcome probabilities are ∣⟨0,ψ⟩∣2=13\abs{\inner0\psi}^2=\tfrac13∣⟨0,ψ⟩∣2=31​ and ∣⟨1,ψ⟩∣2=23\abs{\inner1\psi}^2=\tfrac23∣⟨1,ψ⟩∣2=32​, summing to 111 precisely because Parseval expresses the normalization condition as ∥ψ∥2=13+23=1\norm\psi^2=\tfrac13+\tfrac23=1∥ψ∥2=31​+32​=1.

Example 8.41 (Physics bridge: a time-independent energy basis). For a finite/discrete, time-independent Hamiltonian with orthonormal energy eigenbasis, ∣ψ⟩=∑n⟨En,ψ⟩∣En⟩\ket\psi=\sum_n\inner{E_n}{\psi}\ket{E_n}∣ψ⟩=∑n​⟨En​,ψ⟩∣En​⟩ and the time-evolved state is ∣ψ(t)⟩=∑ne−iEnt/ℏ⟨En,ψ⟩∣En⟩\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\inner{E_n}{\psi}\ket{E_n}∣ψ(t)⟩=∑n​e−iEn​t/ℏ⟨En​,ψ⟩∣En​⟩ (Lecture 6): each coordinate merely rotates by a phase. The probabilities ∣⟨En,ψ⟩∣2\abs{\inner{E_n}{\psi}}^2∣⟨En​,ψ⟩∣2 are unchanged in time, expressing conservation of energy.

Remark 8.42 (Optional extension: three infinite-dimensional hypotheses). Three notions must not be conflated. First, a Hilbert space is an ambient inner-product space complete for its norm; the finite-support space c00c_{00}c00​ is not complete. Second, an orthonormal set is complete when its closed span is the whole Hilbert space; {e1}\set{e_1}{e1​} is orthonormal but not complete in ℓ2\ell^2ℓ2, so Bessel is strict at e2e_2e2​. Third, a subspace must be closed for the Hilbert-space decomposition H=M⊕M⊥H=M\oplus M^\perpH=M⊕M⊥; c00c_{00}c00​ is a dense non-closed subspace of ℓ2\ell^2ℓ2 with c00⊥={0}c_{00}^\perp=\set0c00⊥​={0}. Complete ON sets give expansion/Parseval, and closed subspaces give projection/decomposition. Lecture 13 develops these separate infinite-dimensional theorems.

The remaining ingredient is the adjoint. We have seen (Riesz) that inner products turn vectors into functionals; next lecture we turn operators into their adjoints T†T^{\adj}T† via ⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩, and single out the self-adjoint, unitary, and normal operators—the ones whose eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis, by the spectral theorem of Lecture 10.

Here VVV is finite-dimensional over F∈{R,C}\F\in\set{\R,\C}F∈{R,C}, and every basis sum is finite. ONB coordinates, Parseval, identity resolution, and Gram–Schmidt are finite formulas. For U⊆VU\subseteq VU⊆V, projection gives V=U⊕U⊥V=U\oplus U^\perpV=U⊕U⊥, best approximation, and Bessel. Finite Riesz gives each φ:V→F\varphi:V\to\Fφ:V→F a unique representative, with the field-dependent ket-to-bra behavior.

  • Finite orthonormal basis—finite coordinate, Parseval, and identity-resolution sums.
  • Gram–Schmidt—the finite ONB algorithm, preserving partial spans.
  • Finite projection—V=U⊕U⊥V=U\dsum U^{\perp}V=U⊕U⊥, best approximation, and Bessel.
  • Finite Riesz—unique φ=⟨w,⋅⟩\varphi=\inner{w}{\cdot}φ=⟨w,⋅⟩; ket-to-bra is conjugate-linear over C\CC and linear over R\RR.
