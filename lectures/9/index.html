The Riesz theorem of Lecture 8 turned vectors into functionals. Applied to operators it produces the adjoint T†T^{\adj}T†, the abstract version of the conjugate transpose, defined by ⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩. With the adjoint in hand we can single out the three families of operators that organize the finite-dimensional operator model used in quantum mechanics: the self-adjoint operators T†=TT^{\adj}=TT†=T, whose real eigenvalues label the possible outcomes of an ideal projective measurement; the unitary operators U†=U−1U^{\adj}=U^{-1}U†=U−1, which preserve inner products and implement closed-system time evolution and unitary symmetries (other symmetries may be antiunitary); and the normal operators T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†, the widest class that admits an orthonormal eigenbasis in finite-dimensional complex spaces. This lecture builds the adjoint and proves the spectral facts—real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors, unit-circle spectra—that the spectral theorem of Lecture 10 will complete.

The adjoint

Definition 9.1 (Adjoint). Let T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) on a finite-dimensional inner product space. The adjoint T†T^{\adj}T† is the unique operator satisfying ⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩for all u,v∈V.\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in V.⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩for all u,v∈V.

Uniqueness also proves that these representatives depend linearly on uuu: wu1+u2=wu1+wu2w_{u_1+u_2}=w_{u_1}+w_{u_2}wu1​+u2​​=wu1​​+wu2​​ because both sides represent v↦⟨u1+u2,Tv⟩v\mapsto\inner{u_1+u_2}{Tv}v↦⟨u1​+u2​,Tv⟩. For a∈Fa\in\Fa∈F, the functional for auauau is ϕau=a‾ ϕu\phi_{au}=\conj a\,\phi_uϕau​=aϕu​, while ⟨awu,v⟩=a‾ ⟨wu,v⟩\inner{a w_u}{v}=\conj a\,\inner{w_u}{v}⟨awu​,v⟩=a⟨wu​,v⟩; uniqueness therefore gives wau=awuw_{au}=a w_uwau​=awu​. Self-adjoint operators

Definition 9.11 (Self-adjoint). T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) is self-adjoint (Hermitian) if T†=TT^{\adj}=TT†=T, i.e. ⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{Tu}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}⟨Tu,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩ for all u,vu,vu,v. Its matrix in an orthonormal basis is a Hermitian matrix, A†=AA^{\adj}=AA†=A.

In the finite/discrete pure-point model these operators encode observables. Two facts make them useful there.

Theorem 9.12 (Real eigenvalues). Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.

Proof. Let Tv=λvTv=\lambda vTv=λv with v≠0v\neq0v=0. Then λ∥v∥2=⟨v,Tv⟩=⟨Tv,v⟩=λ‾ ∥v∥2\lambda\norm v^2=\inner{v}{Tv}=\inner{Tv}{v}=\conj\lambda\,\norm v^2λ∥v∥2=⟨v,Tv⟩=⟨Tv,v⟩=λ∥v∥2, using self-adjointness in the middle step. Since ∥v∥2≠0\norm v^2\neq0∥v∥2=0, λ=λ‾\lambda=\conj\lambdaλ=λ is real.

Theorem 9.15 (Orthogonal eigenvectors). Eigenvectors of a self-adjoint operator for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Proof. Let Tu=αuTu=\alpha uTu=αu, Tv=βvTv=\beta vTv=βv with α≠β\alpha\neq\betaα=β (both real). Thus (α−β)⟨u,v⟩=0(\alpha-\beta)\inner{u}{v}=0(α−β)⟨u,v⟩=0, forcing ⟨u,v⟩=0\inner{u}{v}=0⟨u,v⟩=0. 

In the finite ideal-projective model, a measurement of a self-adjoint observable returns one of its real eigenvalues. Distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenspaces and can therefore be distinguished by the corresponding spectral measurement. Continuous-spectrum observables require the projection-valued measures of Lecture 14 instead of ordinary normalizable eigenvectors.

Remark 9.20 (The real case). A real symmetric matrix is self-adjoint for the real dot product, so it too has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigenbasis—the real spectral theorem. This is why the principal axes of an ellipsoid, the moments of inertia of a rigid body, and the normal modes of a coupled system come out mutually perpendicular.

Remark 9.22 (Products of observables). The product of two self-adjoint operators is self-adjoint if and only if they commute, since (ST)†=T†S†=TS(ST)^{\adj}=T^{\adj}S^{\adj}=TS(ST)†=T†S†=TS. A product of observables is again an observable only for compatible (commuting) observables.

Unitary operators

Definition 9.26 (Unitary). U∈L(V)U\in\Lin(V)U∈L(V) is unitary if U†U=UU†=IU^{\adj}U=UU^{\adj}=IU†U=UU†=I, i.e. U†=U−1U^{\adj}=U^{-1}U†=U−1.

Theorem 9.27 (Unitary means inner-product preserving). For U∈L(V)U\in\Lin(V)U∈L(V) the following are equivalent:

  1. UUU is unitary;

  2. ⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩\inner{Uu}{Uv}=\inner{u}{v}⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩ for all u,vu,vu,v (preserves the inner product);

  3. ∥Uv∥=∥v∥\norm{Uv}=\norm v∥Uv∥=∥v∥ for all vvv (preserves norms);

  4. UUU maps some (every) orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis.

Proof. (1)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(2): ⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,U†Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩\inner{Uu}{Uv}=\inner{u}{U^{\adj}Uv}=\inner{u}{v}⟨Uu,Uv⟩=⟨u,U†Uv⟩=⟨u,v⟩. (2)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(3): take u=vu=vu=v. (3)⇒\Rightarrow⇒(2): recover the inner product from the norm by polarization (Lecture 7). Theorem 9.28 (Unit-circle spectrum). Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator has absolute value 111.

Proof. If Uv=λvUv=\lambda vUv=λv with v≠0v\neq0v=0, then ∥v∥=∥Uv∥=∣λ∣ ∥v∥\norm v=\norm{Uv}=\abs\lambda\,\norm v∥v∥=∥Uv∥=∣λ∣∥v∥, so ∣λ∣=1\abs\lambda=1∣λ∣=1; the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, λ=eiθ\lambda=e^{i\theta}λ=eiθ.

Remark 9.33 (Change of orthonormal basis is unitary). The matrix relating two orthonormal bases has orthonormal columns, hence is unitary (Theorem 9.27). Remark 9.34 (Extension: generating unitaries). Normal operators

Definition 9.35 (Normal). T∈L(V)T\in\Lin(V)T∈L(V) is normal if it commutes with its adjoint, T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†.

Self-adjoint operators (T†=TT^{\adj}=TT†=T) and unitary operators (T†=T−1T^{\adj}=T^{-1}T†=T−1) are obviously normal; normality is the common umbrella. Its usefulness rests on one identity.

Theorem 9.36 (Normal length identity). TTT is normal if and only if ∥Tv∥=∥T†v∥\norm{Tv}=\norm{T^{\adj}v}∥Tv∥=​T†v​ for all vvv.

Proof. For any TTT, ∥Tv∥2=⟨Tv,Tv⟩=⟨v,T†Tv⟩\norm{Tv}^2=\inner{Tv}{Tv}=\inner{v}{T^{\adj}Tv}∥Tv∥2=⟨Tv,Tv⟩=⟨v,T†Tv⟩ and ∥T†v∥2=⟨v,TT†v⟩\norm{T^{\adj}v}^2=\inner{v}{TT^{\adj}v}​T†v​2=⟨v,TT†v⟩. These agree for all vvv iff ⟨v,(T†T−TT†)v⟩=0\inner{v}{(T^{\adj}T-TT^{\adj})v}=0⟨v,(T†T−TT†)v⟩=0 for all vvv; as T†T−TT†T^{\adj}T-TT^{\adj}T†T−TT† is self-adjoint, Lemma 9.13 says that holds iff it is 000.

A=(11−11)A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)A=(1−1​11​) has A†=(1−111)A^{\adj}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)A†=(11​−11​) and AA†=A†A=2IAA^{\adj}=A^{\adj}A=2IAA†=A†A=2I, so AAA is normal; yet A†≠AA^{\adj}\neq AA†=A and AA†=2I≠IAA^{\adj}=2I\neq IAA†=2I=I, so it is neither self-adjoint nor unitary. Example 9.39 (Checking normality by lengths). For the same AAA, ∥Av∥2=⟨v,A†Av⟩=2∥v∥2=⟨v,AA†v⟩=∥A†v∥2\norm{Av}^2=\inner{v}{A^{\adj}Av}=2\norm v^2 =\inner{v}{AA^{\adj}v}=\norm{A^{\adj}v}^2∥Av∥2=⟨v,A†Av⟩=2∥v∥2=⟨v,AA†v⟩=​A†v​2, so ∥Av∥=∥A†v∥\norm{Av}=\norm{A^{\adj}v}∥Av∥=​A†v​ for every vvv—the length test of Theorem 9.36, passed.

Remark 9.40 (Positive operators). For any TTT, the operator T†TT^{\adj}TT†T is self-adjoint and positive: ⟨v,T†Tv⟩=∥Tv∥2≥0\inner{v}{T^{\adj}Tv}=\norm{Tv}^2\ge0⟨v,T†Tv⟩=∥Tv∥2≥0, so its eigenvalues are nonnegative. Remark 9.42 (Extension: polar and singular value decompositions). Remark 9.45 (Extension: skew-adjoint operators). Observables, symmetries, and what comes next

For the finite/discrete pure-point model, the dictionary is now complete. A self-adjoint operator plays the role of a real number: its eigenvalues are real (ideal-projective measurement outcomes) and its eigenvectors for distinct outcomes are orthogonal (distinguishable states). A unitary operator plays the role of a complex number of modulus one: it preserves all inner products and its eigenvalues are phases eiθe^{i\theta}eiθ. Both are normal, and normality is exactly the property—by the finite complex spectral theorem of Lecture 10—that guarantees an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Multiplication by xxx on L2[0,1]L^2[0,1]L2[0,1] is the standard warning: it is self-adjoint and normal but has no nonzero ordinary eigenvectors; Lecture 14 replaces finite sums by spectral measures.

In the finite model, self-adjoint observables have orthonormal eigenbases with real eigenvalues, and closed-system evolution is unitary. Unitary symmetries preserve inner products; antiunitary symmetries are the conjugate-linear alternative. The next lecture proves that every normal operator on a finite-dimensional complex space is diagonal in an orthonormal basis.

Proposition 9.47 (Extension: uncertainty relation). For self-adjoint A,BA,BA,B and a unit state ∣ψ⟩\ket\psi∣ψ⟩, writing ΔX=X−⟨X⟩ I\Delta X=X-\expval X\,IΔX=X−⟨X⟩I for X=A,BX=A,BX=A,B, ⟨(ΔA)2⟩ ⟨(ΔB)2⟩≥14∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣2.\expval{(\Delta A)^2}\,\expval{(\Delta B)^2}\ge\tfrac14\abs{\expval{[A,B]}}^2 .⟨(ΔA)2⟩⟨(ΔB)2⟩≥41​∣⟨[A,B]⟩∣2.

Proof. ΔA,ΔB\Delta A,\Delta BΔA,ΔB are self-adjoint, so ⟨(ΔA)2⟩=∥ΔA∣ψ⟩∥2\expval{(\Delta A)^2} =\norm{\Delta A\ket\psi}^2⟨(ΔA)2⟩=∥ΔA∣ψ⟩∥2. Example 9.48 (Incompatible spin components). The Pauli matrices satisfy [σx,σy]=2iσz[\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=2i\sigma_z[σx​,σy​]=2iσz​, so the bound reads ⟨(Δσx)2⟩⟨(Δσy)2⟩≥∣⟨σz⟩∣2\expval{(\Delta\sigma_x)^2}\expval{(\Delta\sigma_y)^2}\ge\abs{\expval{\sigma_z}}^2⟨(Δσx​)2⟩⟨(Δσy​)2⟩≥∣⟨σz​⟩∣2: two spin components cannot both be sharp unless the third has zero expectation. Remark 9.49 (Compatible observables). Commuting finite-dimensional self-adjoint operators can be simultaneously diagonalized in a single orthonormal basis (Lecture 10). In this ideal model, compatible observables share a complete set of eigenstates and can hold definite values together; incompatible ones such as σx\sigma_xσx​ and σz\sigma_zσz​ cannot, which is the origin of the uncertainty relation.

The adjoint T†T^{\adj}T†, defined by ⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner{u}{Tv}⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩, is the conjugate transpose in an orthonormal basis and obeys (ST)†=T†S†(ST)^{\adj}=T^{\adj}S^{\adj}(ST)†=T†S†, (T†)†=T(T^{\adj})^{\adj}=T(T†)†=T, with null⁡T†=(range⁡T)⊥\nul T^{\adj}=(\rng T)^\perpnullT†=(rangeT)⊥. Self-adjoint operators (T†=TT^{\adj}=TT†=T) have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors—the observables. Unitary operators (U†=U−1U^{\adj}=U^{-1}U†=U−1) preserve inner products and norms, map orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases, and have eigenvalues on the unit circle—the unitary symmetries and closed-system evolutions. Antiunitary symmetries are a separate conjugate-linear possibility. Normal operators (T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†) satisfy ∥Tv∥=∥T†v∥\norm{Tv}=\norm{T^{\adj}v}∥Tv∥=​T†v​, share eigenvectors with T†T^{\adj}T† (conjugate eigenvalues), and have orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues; in finite-dimensional complex spaces they are exactly the operators the spectral theorem diagonalizes in an orthonormal basis.

  • Adjoint—⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩\inner{T^{\adj}u}{v}=\inner u{Tv}⟨T†u,v⟩=⟨u,Tv⟩; the conjugate transpose in an orthonormal basis; null⁡T†=(range⁡T)⊥\nul T^{\adj}=(\rng T)^{\perp}nullT†=(rangeT)⊥.

  • Unitary (U†=U−1U^{\adj}=U^{-1}U†=U−1)—closed-system evolution and unitary symmetries; antiunitary symmetries are conjugate-linear; unitary spectrum lies on the unit circle.
  • Normal (T†T=TT†T^{\adj}T=TT^{\adj}T†T=TT†)—commutes with its adjoint, so ∥Tv∥=∥T†v∥\norm{Tv}=\norm{T^{\adj}v}∥Tv∥=​T†v​; orthogonal eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues, the finite complex spectral-theorem class.
